# Apong among the Mising — Assam research dossier

- **Archive IDs:** ASM-002-NOG (Noggin Apong); ASM-003-POR (Poro Apong / Saimod)
- **State:** Assam
- **Community:** Mising
- **Category:** Traditional rice-based alcoholic beverages
- **Starter culture:** ÉPOP / Epop / Epop pitha (spellings vary by source)
- **Evidence status:** Core distinction and starter-culture tradition documented; current village-by-village coverage incomplete.
- **Important distinction:** Published descriptions distinguish Noggin/Nagin/Nogin Apong from Poro/Por/Po:ro Apong. Poro Apong is also called Saimod in some research. The names should be verified with community/language sources before being treated as exact synonyms.

## Evidence-backed overview

Apong is a culturally significant rice-based alcoholic beverage tradition among the Mising community. A 2023 open-access study by Mili and Sundriyal documented ÉPOP starter-culture practices in Sivasagar district. It surveyed 15 villages and selected Majorbari and Ligiribari for in-depth study; it records 31 plant species across 22 families used in the studied starter-culture tradition. That inventory is specific to the sampled research context and must not be treated as a universal recipe for all Mising communities.

## Forms currently tracked

### Noggin Apong — ASM-002-NOG
The 2023 study describes Noggin/Nogin Apong as a whitish liquid extracted from fermented material, with rice used and no ash addition in the distinction it presents. Other studies use the spelling Nagin. The archive treats these as likely spelling variants pending confirmation.

### Poro Apong / Saimod — ASM-003-POR
Research describes Poro/Por/Po:ro Apong, also called Saimod in some sources, as a rice-based beverage with ash from rice husks or bran added to cooked rice before fermentation. **The post-fermentation process is contested in the literature:** some sources describe distillation, while the full-text Majuli 2026 study (SRC-084) describes water extraction and filtration through a conical bamboo Ta:suk basket, without a distillation stage in that account. These are not interchangeable operations. The archive now preserves both descriptions as source-specific and does not resolve the product classification until the original methods and local terminology are reconciled.

## Starter culture: ÉPOP / Epop

The 2023 Sivasagar study documents plant materials collected from several land-use settings, dried and crushed, then mixed with soaked glutinous rice to form starter cakes. It describes inoculation with material from an earlier starter batch and drying/storage practices. The paper reports 31 plant species across 22 families and states that the cakes in that studied context could be stored up to six months.

**Caution:** Do not copy the 31-species inventory into a single universal list for the whole Mising population. Ingredient selection, local plant names, ecological access and preparation practices can vary. Any claimed medicinal benefit should be attributed as traditional use rather than treated as established clinical efficacy.

- **Ligiribari, Sivasagar district:** explicitly named as a representative village for in-depth ÉPOP starter-culture research.
- **Barmukuli, Dhemaji district:** a locality lead associated with published Apong ethnobotanical literature; verify the exact scope and claims in the full article before asserting beverage-form-specific production.
- **Majuli district:** a 2022 study documents homemade Mising beverage forms and associated ethnomedicinal practices; the current archive needs to extract any named villages.
- **Lakhimpur district:** a multi-community survey includes a Mising Apong section; named locality and current production details need further verification.

These records do not establish that Apong is made in every Mising village or that the same form and recipe occur throughout these districts.

## Cultural context

The literature describes Apong as part of hospitality, social life and some ritual/community events, including events connected with Mising agricultural and festival life. Specific customs, gathering restrictions and gendered knowledge roles described in one study should remain attributed to that study and locality; do not generalise them to every Mising household.

## Editorial rule

Treat this as a living research dossier. Keep the beverage forms distinct where their processes differ, cite source IDs, preserve local spellings, and distinguish research locality from confirmed present-day production locality.

### Comparative field study lead: SRC-061

Borah et al. (2021) include Mising communities in a multi-community ethnobotanical field study covering 32 villages across six Assam districts and 202 respondents overall. The authors specifically distinguish Po:ro apong as having a different preparation/extraction process and report distillation in the sampled Mising tradition. These are study-level findings; do not assume the abstract establishes a universal method or current production in every sampled village. The full tables and locality map should be checked before assigning specific plant inventories to individual places. [SRC-061](https://doi.org/10.13057/biodiv/d220451)

### Methodological scope of the 2021 multi-community study

The full-text methods section specifies fieldwork from **October 2019 to March 2020** in 32 villages across **Baksa, Biswanath, Golaghat, Lakhimpur, Sivasagar (printed as Sibasagar) and Sonitpur**. The 202 respondents covered Ahom, Bodo, Karbi and Mising communities. The main text does not map each Mising product observation to an individual village; do not interpret the six overall study districts as confirmed production localities for Nogin Apong or Po:ro Apong. The paper does specifically distinguish Po:ro Apong's ash-assisted preparation and Ta:suk bamboo-basket extraction process from the more general rice-beer method, but this description remains study-level and should not replace locality-specific accounts. [SRC-061](https://doi.org/10.13057/biodiv/d220451)

## Full-text review: Majuli ethnobotanical study (SRC-084)

Borah (2026), *International Journal of Advanced Research* 14(4):1361–1368, reports a field survey in **three randomly selected villages in Majuli from April to October 2024**, involving **28 informants**. The accessible full-text mirror does not name the three villages, so no village names have been inferred or added. The study documents 48 plant species used in preparing E’pob starter cakes; it identifies rice (*Oryza sativa*) and Rukji (*Dryopteris filix-mas*) among the highest reported relative-frequency/use-value entries. These results describe the sampled Majuli context, not a universal Mising recipe.

### Starter-cake process reported in SRC-084

The source describes cleaning and soaking rice for approximately 2–3 hours, pounding it into powder, drying and pounding collected plant leaves, mixing plant powder with rice and a small amount of an older starter culture, shaping cakes, placing them on a bamboo sieve with paddy straw and Rukji leaves, sun-drying for 2–3 days, and storing the cakes in an earthen pot plugged with Rukji leaves. It reports that cakes can be stored in a dry place for 1–2 months. This method is now represented in the production-method table as source-specific entries ASM-MTH-035 and ASM-MTH-038.

### Nogin Apong method reported in SRC-084

The study describes cooked rice cooled on a bamboo mat or banana leaves, mixed with powdered E’pob and transferred to an earthen pot. It reports fermentation for 7–10 days; a portion of the fermented rice is mixed with water, stirred until milky and sieved to obtain the beverage. This is one study's method, not a universal fermentation schedule or food-safety validation. It is recorded as ASM-MTH-036.

### Po:ro Apong method reported in SRC-084

The study describes partially burning dry paddy straw and paddy husk to collect ash, mixing the ash with cooked rice and powdered E’pob, and fermenting the mixture in an earthen pot. It then describes transferring the fermented mixture to a conical bamboo **Ta:suk** basket lined with leaves and straw, adding water in stages and collecting the filtrate. The source reports roughly one to two weeks for fermentation depending on starter quantity and warmer/cooler conditions. It does **not** describe distillation as part of this Po:ro-specific method. The 2021 study likewise describes Po:ro filtration in its specific section but discusses distillation separately; the 2023 paper explicitly calls Poro distilled. Whether these descriptions refer to an additional stage, distinct variant/product, or a reporting/terminology issue remains unresolved. The method is recorded as ASM-MTH-037.

### Evidence cautions

- The article describes ethnomedicinal knowledge and plant use; those claims do not establish clinical efficacy or safety.
- The three Majuli villages are unnamed in the accessible text; the archive adds ASM-PL-078 only as a study-scope record.
- The Jorhat 2026 study names ten villages (records ASM-PL-068–ASM-PL-077) but does not map each beverage to each village in the material reviewed. Do not fill the beverage IDs on those individual village rows without direct evidence.
- SRC-084's preparation details have been added to the production-method table; no ingredient inventory is generalized to all Mising households.

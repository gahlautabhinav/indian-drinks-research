# SRC-113 — Zao, purification and religious change among Zeme Nagas

## Citation
Longkumer, A. (2016). “Rice-Beer, Purification and Debates over Religion and Culture in Northeast India.” *South Asia: Journal of South Asian Studies*, 39(2), 444–461. https://doi.org/10.1080/00856401.2016.1154645

## Source type and scope
Peer-reviewed ethnographic analysis based on Longkumer's fieldwork among Zeme Nagas in North Cachar Hills (now Dima Hasao, Assam). The article centres on a village rendered as “Hsongle”; the author states that, apart from Haflong, place and personal names are pseudonyms. The fieldwork described began in 2005. The article is about rice-beer's religious, social and political meanings, not a representative prevalence survey or a controlled fermentation study.

## Beverage terminology and preparation details
- The article uses the spelling **zao** for Zeme rice-beer.
- It distinguishes **Herazao** (“God's zao”) from **Tekuizao** or **Zao kesang**, which it describes as grainy and less alcoholic and as a rice-only preparation with no added fermenting agent. It also mentions **Klezao**, described as watered-down rice-beer, particularly consumed by children in the reported context.
- These names are not a direct one-to-one match with Atungbou's 2020 spellings **Nduizou/Herazou** and **Tekuizou/Zousang**. The similarity is a valuable crosswalk lead, not proof of equivalence: retain each author's spelling and source-specific definition until community or author confirmation.
- Longkumer recounts a Zeme origin narrative involving rice, banana leaf and liquid from tree bark called “nduihi gei.” This is a cultural narrative, not evidence that this bark is a starter ingredient in every contemporary preparation.
- The account distinguishes local religious practices and Christian denominations in their relationships to zao. It should not be flattened into a universal statement about all Zeme people or current practice.

## Evidence limitations and Atlas decisions
- The focal village name is pseudonymous, so do not add “Hsongle” as a verified real-world village or map it to a specific settlement.
- The paper supports a separate ethnographic source record for Zao/Zao-related forms and social context. It does not supply a current statewide distribution, household counts, recipe standard, analytical alcohol measurement, or sample-level laboratory data.
- Do not merge SRC-113's Herazao with Atungbou's Herazou/Nduizou, or merge Tekuizao with Tekuizou/Zousang, without checking whether spellings, referents and preparation steps are equivalent in community usage.
- Religious descriptions reflect the studied context and period; avoid treating them as timeless or universal.
- Cultural narratives and reported medicinal meanings are not clinical efficacy or food-safety evidence.

## Cross-source synthesis
- **Longkumer (2016; SRC-113):** Zao; Herazao; Tekuizao/Zao kesang; Klezao; “nduihi gei” in an origin narrative.
- **Atungbou (2020; SRC-111):** Zou umbrella; Tekuizou/Zousang; Tamzou/Lingtakzou; Nduizou/Herazou; starter called nduihi in the beverage description.
- **Yumnam et al. (2024; SRC-110):** Zao in prose/methods, Zou in Table 1; starter Ndhuihi; three cohort samples, no named village.
- **Yumnam, Nath & Sharma (2026; SRC-112):** household ethnography across three districts and five communities; accessible publisher metadata does not expose the sample-level tables.
These sources use overlapping forms, but do not establish that all forms are synonyms. The “nduihi” term in Longkumer's origin narrative and the starter name “nduihi” in Atungbou should also not be assumed to refer to the same material without clarification.

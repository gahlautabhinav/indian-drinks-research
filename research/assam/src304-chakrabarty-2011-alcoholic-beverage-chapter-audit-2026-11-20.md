# SRC-304 full-text audit — North Cachar Hills traditional alcoholic beverages

**Review date:** 20 November 2026  
**Source:** Jayasree Chakrabarty, G. D. Sharma & Jyoti Prakash Tamang (2011), “Traditional Knowledge of Ethnic People of North Cachar Hills District of Assam on Fermentation of Alcoholic Beverages,” Chapter 16 in *Status and Conservation of Bio-diversity in North-East India*. Full-text PDF: https://www.bilaspuruniversity.ac.in/PDF/ResearchPublications/197.pdf  
**Related records:** SRC-303 (2011 thesis lead), SRC-097 (2009 paper), SRC-098 (2014 paper), SRC-109 (2017 Nduiyi/Nduijao paper), ASM-GAP-124.

## Executive finding

This chapter is a substantial primary documentation lead, not merely a bibliographic reference. It provides a district-level inventory of named alcoholic beverages among several North Cachar Hills communities and process descriptions for Judima, Juharo, Dekuijao, Nduijao bung, Zunak/Zuhing, Remalu's Baitui and Bumong's Baitui. It does not give named field villages, producer counts, survey dates or a sample schedule. Treat every process as the authors' historical district-level account, not a universal or current recipe.

## 1. Community–beverage inventory

The chapter's table associates:
- **Dimasa:** Judima (rice beer), Juharo (distilled liquor), plus Miyamikhri (bamboo shoot product, not a beverage).
- **Jeme Naga:** Dekujiao and Nduijao (both rice beers).
- **Rengmai Naga:** Bhodka (rice beer), Cholai (distilled liquor).
- **Karbi:** Zu (rice beer).
- **Kuki:** Zu (rice beer).
- **Baite:** Remalu's Baitui and Bumong's Baitui (rice beers).
- **Hrangkol:** the extracted table appears to list “Saphak Zunak” and “Zuhning” as rice beer, followed by an anomalous “Pork fat / Rice” row. Cross-checking SRC-305 shows Saphak is fermented pork fat, not a beverage; therefore “Saphak Zunak” is treated as a likely table-layout/transcription ambiguity, not a confirmed compound beverage name. Zunak is separately listed as a North Cachar Hills rice beer in SRC-097, but its community is not resolved by the accessible abstract.
- **Vaiphee:** Bekanthu (soybean), Sathu (pork/beef fat), Tuithuok (bamboo shoot), Sithu (sesame), Ankam thu (mustard leaf), Aithu (crab). These are fermented foods, not alcoholic beverages.
- **Khelma:** beverage/product not known in table.

This is a reported historical inventory, not evidence that all named products remain in production or are present across the entire district.

## 2. Starter-culture account — do not over-normalise

The chapter describes Biron rice soaked, crushed with *Glycyrrhiza glabra* bark, mixed with water into paste, shaped into cakes and air-dried. The prose gives 10–12 hours soaking, while the flow sheet gives 6–8 hours. It then says Dimasa call the starter Humao and Jeme Naga call it Nduhi.

This generic account is not automatically equivalent to Humao in the Judima/Humao literature (SRC-098), Nduiyi in Chakrabarty 2017 (SRC-109), or Zeme Nduihi/Ndhuihi in SRC-110/SRC-111. SRC-109 gives a separate starter recipe using *Albizia myriophylla* bark or *Saccharum officinarum* leaves. Keep both source accounts intact pending community and author clarification.

## 3. Process accounts

### Judima (Dimasa)
Rice is cleaned, washed, cooked and dewatered, cooled moderately, mixed with Humao, spread on banana leaf overnight, then transferred to a partially airtight earthen pot. The chapter reports 3–4 days fermentation in summer and 6–7 days in winter; expressed juice is called Judima. This is a historical district-level account, not a replacement for the village-specific Dhansiri study in SRC-001.

### Juharo (Dimasa distilled liquor)
Cooked Biron rice is mixed with Humao and fermented 5–6 days. It is filtered through cotton cloth; the filtrate is boiled and the distillate collected in a small vessel and bottled. No distillation apparatus, temperature, cut fractions or safety controls are described. Do not present this as a validated production or safety protocol.

### Dekuijao / Dekujiao (Jeme Naga)
The process heading spells Dekuijao; the opening table spells Dekujiao. Paddy is soaked and wrapped above and below with banana leaves in a container called Ntui; after three days it sprouts and is called Rehi. Rehi is partially sun-dried, ground and sieved repeatedly to remove husk. The powder is mixed with lukewarm water in a bamboo container called Kepup using a Hetatang bamboo stick; cold water is added, the mixture transferred to Keladia and closed with cloth for three days. The chapter says it is ready for consumption. It separately says Naga people mix Dekujiao with milk. The exact role of Ntui and whether Keladia is a basket or container needs terminology review.

### Nduijao bung (Jeme Naga)
The process heading is “Nduijao bung.” Biron rice is cooked, sometimes with cultivated crop seeds, spread on a flat bamboo pan, mixed with Nduhi starter and transferred to a vessel covered with banana leaf and tightly closed. It is kept near the fireplace for 2–3 days and filtered through muslin/cotton cloth; the filtrate is called Nduijao. The prose says “vessel”; the flow sheet specifies a gourd vessel. The chapter does not explain what “bung” means or identify the cultivated seeds.

**Critical cross-source issue:** this is a finished-beverage process absent from SRC-109, but the starter is called Nduhi rather than Nduiyi. Do not replace the 2017 starter recipe or assert synonymy without confirmation.

### Zunak and Zuhing / Zuhning (Hrangkol table attribution)
Biron rice is cooked and mixed with Humao, then kept 7–8 days in a gourd or an earthen pot with a lower outlet. The liquid emerging from the lower opening is called Zuhing; residual rice is called Zunak and is served after mixing with water. The extracted opening table appears to list “Saphak Zunak” and Zuhning under Hrangkol, but SRC-305 explicitly identifies Saphak as fermented pork fat among Hrangkol (and Sathu among Vaipei). The 2009 article abstract separately lists Zunak as a North Cachar Hills rice beer, without community attribution in the accessible excerpt. Treat the apparent “Saphak Zunak” row as a table alignment/transcription issue until the original print page is inspected. The process text does not restate the community. The liquid and residual rice are distinct outputs; whether Zuhing is a separately consumed beverage remains unresolved.

### Remalu's Baitui (Baite)
The chapter describes Remalu's Chol starter: local crop seeds called Remalu are soaked, placed in a Khamulua basket to sprout for three days, sun-dried and mixed with wet rice into a paste. For Baitui, Biron rice is ground, soaked overnight, husk removed by sieving, mixed with starter and lukewarm water, stirred with Hetatang, poured into an Um/Lao vessel, sealed and kept near the fireplace for three days. The plant/crop identity and the relationship between Um and Lao are not supplied.

### Bumong's Baitui (Baite)
Paddy is soaked and sprouted, then mixed with banana leaf, sugarcane and an unidentified creeper leaf, powdered, placed on a flat bamboo drying pan covered with banana leaf and shade-dried to make Bumong Chol. Cooked Biron rice is spread on Rilei, mixed with Chol, transferred to Lao, sealed for three days and filtered through muslin. The filtrate is called Bumong Baitui or Zu. The unidentified creeper leaf must remain unidentified; this Zu must not be merged with Karbi or Kuki Zu.

## 4. Social and economic statements

The chapter says rice beer is offered during harvest festivals, marriages and Shradh ceremonies; it describes a practice in which a newly married bride carries Zu when visiting and returning from her parents' home. It states that preparation is household-based, women prepare beverages and men help pound soaked rice, and that Zu and starter cultures are sold in markets, food stalls and restaurants. These are broad author-reported observations without named village or respondent data. They must not be universalised or treated as present-day prevalence evidence.

## 5. Methodological limitations

No fieldwork dates, villages, sample counts, named producers, interview schedule or community-by-product allocation; no quantitative ingredient ratios, alcohol values, microbiological data or botanical vouchers. Generic starter account has conflicting soaking times (10–12 h prose; 6–8 h flow sheet). Nduhi vs Nduiyi remains unresolved. Dekujiao/Dekuijao and Zuhning/Zuhing spellings vary. “Pork fat” appears as a row in the beverage inventory table with rice substrate, likely a table-layout or transcription issue; do not create it as a beverage. Chapter pagination varies across citations: extracted PDF body shows book pages 202–209; the 2017 paper cites pp. 200–211; an academic CV lists pp. 210–221. Exact edition pagination needs title/copyright pages and contents. The chapter may overlap with the 2011 thesis and other Chakrabarty publications, but overlap is not proven.

## 8. Cross-source correction entered on 20 November 2026

Das & Deka (2012; SRC-305) explicitly describe **Saphak** as fermented pork fat among Hrangkol and **Sathu** as fermented pork fat among Vaipei (pp. 390–391 in the review's printed pagination). This conflicts with a literal reading of the extracted 2011 table row “Saphak Zunak (rice beer)”. The 2009 Chakrabarty et al. abstract separately lists **Zunak** as a North Cachar Hills rice beer. Accordingly, the archive now records **Zunak** alone and does not treat “Saphak Zunak” as a confirmed compound name. The Hrangkol assignment of Zunak and the relationship between Zuhning (table) and Zuhing (process text) remain unresolved. No Saphak beverage record was created.

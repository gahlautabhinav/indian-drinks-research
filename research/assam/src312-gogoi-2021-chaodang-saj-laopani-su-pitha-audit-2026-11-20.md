**Audit date:** 20 November 2026  
**Citation:** Girish Gogoi (2021), “Traditional knowledge on preparation of starter culture cakes and rice-beers by Chaodang community of upper Assam, India,” *Emergent Life Sciences Research* 7(1):63–75. DOI: 10.31783/elsr.2021.716375.

## 1. Research design and locality evidence

The author reports a field survey in ten villages in Baghchung Development Block, Jorhat district, Assam. Eight key persons, mainly experienced older men and women, were interviewed in each village: **80 traditional knowledge holders in total**. The villages were selected randomly. Prior informed consent was obtained from knowledge holders, village heads (Gaon Burha) and/or Gaon Panchayat presidents. The author says the preparation processes were recorded and photographed with permission, plant samples were collected and identified using botanical references, and GPS coordinates were recorded for each village.

The ten named villages are:

1. Habungia Gaon
2. Jamuguri Gaon
3. Kumar Chaodang Gaon
4. Borbheta Gaon
5. Kebang Gaon
6. Baghmoria Gaon
7. Timtimia Gaon
8. Lojonia Gaon
9. Ujani Nonoi Gaon
10. Khoriachuk Gaon

The paper includes a sampling-area map (Figure 1), but the coordinates are not tabulated in the extracted text. It does not provide an individual household or informant ID crosswalk to a specific recipe. These ten villages are therefore survey localities for the Chaodang starter/beverage tradition; the paper does not establish that every rice variety, every starter plant, or every process variant occurred in every village.

The author describes Chaodang populations beyond Jorhat in other Upper Assam districts, but this study's ten-village sample is specifically from Baghchung Development Block, Jorhat. Do not extend the ten-village sample to Golaghat, Sivasagar, Lakhimpur or Dibrugarh.

## 2. Names and product boundaries

The paper describes the beverage as **Saj or Laopani** for the Chaodang community, made using starter cakes called **Su-pitha / Modor-pitha**. It separately names two extraction stages:

- **Rohi mod** — the first extraction from the fermented rice mass.
- **Choka mod** — the second extraction after adding more water to the remaining fermented mass; described as white, more turbid and lower in alcohol than the first extraction.

These names should not be collapsed into synonyms. In this archive, the canonical beverage row is Saj (Chaodang); Laopani is retained as a source-used alternate label, while Rohi mod and Choka mod are documented as extraction fractions/variants. Do not merge this Chaodang Laopani with Ahom Sajpani or other communities' Laopani without independent terminology evidence.

## 3. Rice varieties

The paper reports three rice categories used in Saj preparation:

- Bora rice (sticky/glutinous rice)
- Chakuwa rice
- Normal rice, identified as Sali variety
- Sometimes a mixture of all three

The article qualitatively ranks Bora rice beverage most favourably for taste, sweetness, yellow colour and aroma; a mixture is described as next best; Chakuwa is described as having high alcohol concentration; normal Sali rice as paler, slightly bitter and lower in alcohol. These are qualitative author-reported sensory observations, not measured alcohol percentages or controlled comparative chemistry.

## 4. Su-pitha / Modor-pitha starter preparation

The source describes the following process:

1. Starter plants are collected from homestead gardens, tea gardens, private forests, roadsides, riversides, agricultural fields and natural forests.
2. *Amblovenatum opulentum* is described as the largest-quantity plant and is sun-dried first: 1–2 days in summer or 3–4 days in winter. Other plant materials are generally kept fresh because drying them makes pounding difficult.
3. Normal Sali rice is soaked for 3–4 hours, drained for about one hour and washed.
4. Rice and plant materials are pounded together in a **Dheki** (wooden mortar).
5. The mixture is sieved through a bamboo sieve and then a finer metal sieve, repeatedly, until powdered.
6. Clean water is added to make a paste. Round cakes of about 2.5 cm diameter are formed.
7. Each fresh cake is coated with powder from a selected old Su-pitha cake, which serves as the back-slopping inoculum.
8. Cakes are covered above and below with paddy straw for four days in summer or six days in winter. The source describes a white surface growth.
9. Cakes are air-dried for 2–3 hours, then placed on a bamboo frame called **Dhua chang** or a bamboo sieve over the kitchen fire. Smoke/heat dries them in 2–3 days.
10. The dried starter cakes are ready for use or storage; the paper reports usual storage of 2–3 months.

The paper does not give exact mass ratios for the 81 plant species, nor does it provide a standardized recipe for every village. Do not turn this description into a universal formula.

## 5. Saj / Laopani fermentation and extraction

The article reports:

1. Wash and cook the selected rice variety. Bora rice is cooked in boiling-water steam, as described by the author.
2. Spread the cooked rice on banana leaves and cool for about one hour.
3. Crush Su-pitha and mix at **3–5 starter cakes per kg of rice**.
4. Cover the inoculated rice with banana leaves and allow an initial fermentation of **1–2 days in summer** or **3–4 days in winter**.
5. Transfer the fermented mixture to a smoked and dried earthen pot and continue fermentation for **5–7 days**.
6. Add approximately **1.5 litres of clean water per kg of rice** to the pot. After 12 hours, pour off or sieve the first extraction, called **Rohi mod**.
7. Add approximately **1 litre of water to the remaining fermented mass per kg**, mix and sieve a second time to obtain **Choka mod**.
8. The paper describes Choka mod as white, more turbid and lower in alcohol. The remaining solid mass is reported as animal feed.

The paper says the process is kept away from citrus plants because the author reports they can make the product sour. This is a reported traditional practice, not a controlled experiment proving a general citrus-avoidance mechanism.

**No alcohol concentration was measured or reported** for Saj, Rohi mod or Choka mod in this field survey. Do not import alcohol percentages from Bodo Emao/Jou papers or other communities.

## 6. Plant diversity in Su-pitha

The publisher PDF reports **81 plant species in 47 families** in Table 1 and the body text. The paper states:
- 50 species were common to all ten surveyed villages.
- The remaining 31 were not common to all villages.
- One village used as many as 61 species.
- The 81 taxa are the total documented across the ten-village Jorhat sample, not 81 species in every starter cake.
- Plant parts recorded include leaves (46 species), whole plants (19), fronds (6), leaves and bark (2), young shoots (2), and several other part categories.

**Safety and evidence boundary:** the paper's designation “medicinal plants” is an ethnobotanical description. It does not establish clinical efficacy, safe oral doses or safety of every listed species. Some listed taxa (including *Croton tiglium* and *Cannabis sativa*) warrant specialist toxicological and legal review. The list is not a recipe or a recommendation to ingest or cultivate any plant. The article does not state that every plant is used in every cake or every village.

## 7. Internal count discrepancy

The publisher-hosted PDF's abstract, Table 1 and body state **81 species**. A secondary Academia.edu text/metadata extraction displays “51 plants” in an abstract excerpt, likely a transcription/indexing error. The original publisher PDF is the controlling source for the archive entry, but the mismatch is retained in ASM-GAP-131 for clarification. The audit does not use the secondary “51” count.

## 8. Cultural and current-practice claims

The author reports Saj/Laopani preparation for Bihu, ritual functions, birth and marriage ceremonies, and offerings to ancestors. The paper also reports declining knowledge/availability of some plant species and that some younger people are less familiar with them. These are author-reported findings from the 2020 field survey, not a current 2026 prevalence survey.

The article makes health, energy and “hallucination” statements about Saj. These are not clinical findings and should not be repeated as established effects. It also mentions historical prices for starter cakes and rice beer; these are 2020-era observations and are not current market prices.

1. Recover the Figure 1 GPS locations or ask the author for the original coordinate sheet/GIS layer.
2. Request village-wise plant-use matrices, plant voucher/accession details and photographs/field notebook references.
3. Confirm current Chaodang terminology for Saj, Laopani, Rohi mod and Choka mod with community knowledge holders.

**Conclusion:** SRC-312 is a high-value field source because it names ten survey villages, reports 80 key informants and prior informed consent, records GPS use, and documents a detailed Chaodang starter and beverage process. It also records a remarkably broad plant inventory. Its limits are the absence of village-specific recipes, GPS coordinate table, plant voucher identifiers and measured beverage chemistry. Keep the full 81-taxon list at source level until those provenance gaps are resolved.

### Tranche 1 exceptions and interpretation

Four entries in this first tranche have explicit POWO name-resolution issues: entry 1 (*Phlogacanthus thyrsiflorus*), entry 9 (*Hydrocotyle sibthorpioides* without author citation), entry 16 (*Merremia umbellata*) and entry 18 (*Cheilocostus speciosus*). Entry 9 is particularly important: POWO's accepted *Hydrocotyle sibthorpioides* Lam. is not the same nomenclatural record as the later homonymous *H. sibthorpioides* Colenso, which POWO synonymises under *H. moschata*. POWO also places *Blechnum orientale* and *Stenochlaena palustris* in Aspleniaceae. Fern family circumscriptions have changed across classifications; this current backbone placement should not be treated as evidence that the article's family column (if transcribed from an older flora) was necessarily an identification error.

**Scope limit:** this tranche covers entries 1–20 only. It does not yet reconcile the article's printed family column, vernacular names or plant parts row by row, and it does not authenticate field specimens. Entries 21–81 remain pending.

### Tranche 2 interpretation

Two clear name-level changes occur in this tranche: *Desmodium laxiflorum* is treated by POWO as a synonym of *Sohmaea laxiflora*, and the source's *Lindernia ruellioides* is associated with POWO's *Lindernia ruelloides* synonym record under *Bonnaya ruelloides*. The latter is not a simple typo correction: POWO itself records regional works that used the spelling *Lindernia ruellioides* under an alternative taxonomy. Keep the source spelling and record the alternative treatment.

The *Spatholobus* entry is only “sp.”; the fact that POWO recognises *Spatholobus suberectus* as a species does not justify assigning that epithet to this specimen. A genus-level entry must remain genus-level until a voucher, photograph or diagnostic description supports identification.

*Cuphea carthagenensis* is accepted by POWO, but POWO records Assam as an introduced range. This is a biogeographic caution, not a reason to remove it from the ethnobotanical list: an introduced plant may be locally available and used. The paper's record does not establish when or how it arrived, whether it is naturalised at the collection sites, or the identity of the actual specimen.

### High-priority identity flags from tranche 3

Three records deserve more than routine synonym mapping:

1. **Entry 44 — *Cissampelos sympodialis*.** POWO accepts this name but places its native range in eastern Brazil. That is a strong biogeographic mismatch for a Jorhat ethnobotanical survey. A review of the Indian herbaceous *Phyllanthus* complex states that true *P. niruri* does not occur in India and that older Indian reports under this name commonly refer to *P. amarus*, *P. fraternus* and/or *P. debilis*. ## 16. Functional-role evidence audit — Su-pitha plant ingredients versus fermentation biology (21 November 2026)

### 16.1 What SRC-312 actually documents

Gogoi (2021) makes four different kinds of statements that must not be collapsed into one:

1. **Relative quantity:** six taxa are explicitly said to be required in larger quantities than the other ingredients: *Amblovenatum opulentum*, *Lygodium microphyllum*, *Lygodium flexuosum*, *Mussaenda frondosa*, *Centella asiatica* and *Hydrocotyle sibthorpioides*. The paper says *A. opulentum* is required in the maximum amount. This is a reported Chaodang formulation observation, not a measured mass/percentage recipe.
2. **Processing treatment:** *A. opulentum* is sun-dried separately for 1–2 days in summer or 3–4 days in winter. The other plant materials are not dried because the author reports that drying makes them difficult to grind in the dheki. This is a preparation/handling distinction, not proof of a biochemical role.
3. **Broad attributed functions:** the paper says the 81 taxa have distinct roles and that some are additives, some contribute sweet taste, and some provide “stimulation”. It does **not** map those three functions to individual taxa, define “stimulation”, quantify sensory effects, or provide a plant-omission/add-back experiment. Do not assign these functions to specific CSV rows.
4. **Starter inoculation and fermentation:** the new cakes are overlaid with powder from selected old Su-pitha, described as the source of yeast/inoculum. The white surface after incubation is described as yeast growth. The paper does not report isolation, sequencing, counts or enzyme assays from Chaodang Su-pitha. Its description of starch conversion (diastase/maltase) and subsequent ethanol/CO2 production (zymase) is a process explanation, not a biochemical measurement in this study.

### 16.3 What related Assam microbiology studies can—and cannot—tell us

- **Ahom Xaj-pitha (2016):** a metagenomic study of an Ahom starter reports organisms with amylolytic potential, including *Rhizopus delemar*, *Mucor circinelloides* and *Aspergillus* sp.; it also reports several yeast taxa associated with ethanol production and a diverse bacterial community including lactic-acid bacteria. This is direct microbial evidence for the sampled **Ahom Xaj-pitha**, not Chaodang Su-pitha and not a plant-by-plant mechanism. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4947050/
- **Bodo emao (2021):** shotgun metagenomics reports a fungal-dominated community, especially Rhizopus/Rhizopodaceae, and metabolic modules/enzymes associated with saccharification and related processes. Emao is a distinct Bodo starter. It provides a useful comparative hypothesis for a Chaodang study, not an identification of Su-pitha organisms. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8285430/
- **Assam rice-beer mycobiology (Saikia et al., 2024):** reports 16 yeast isolates and two filamentous fungi from nine starter samples, with identifications including *Aspergillus oryzae*, *A. niger*, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, *Saccharomycopsis fibuligera* and *Candida tropicalis*. The accessible abstract does not provide a reliable Chaodang/Su-pitha sample attribution; do not assign these isolates to ASM-039-SAJ. https://doi.org/10.18805/ajdfr.DR-1829
- **Assam beverage microbiota/metabolites (2019):** the study examines Apong (Mising), Xaaj (Ahom) and Joubishi (Bodo), not Chaodang Saj. It is useful for designing comparative microbial/metabolite work but cannot stand in for a Chaodang batch profile. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6458194/
- **Plant-leaf phytochemistry (2015):** a separate Assam study tests extracts from four plant leaves used in rice-beer starter preparation—*Artocarpus heterophyllus*, *Cinnamomum extensa*, *Oroxylum corymbosum* and *Alpinia malaccensis*—for phytochemical composition and antimicrobial activity. It explicitly describes the leaves as incorporated into starter cakes rather than directly added to the fermentation mash. The result is evidence about those extracts under that study's assay conditions, not proof that these plants control Chaodang Su-pitha microbes or that their extracts are safe/beneficial in beverage consumption. https://doi.org/10.1080/10942912.2015.1104695
- **2024 Northeast India review:** useful as a secondary map of reported beverage/starter/plant literature, but its compiled therapeutic claims are not Chaodang-specific causal evidence and should be traced to primary sources before use. https://doi.org/10.1155/2024/7769743

| Finished-beverage chemical/ethanol profile linked to plant composition | Not established by SRC-312 | No numeric composition or plant-causality claims |

### 16.5 Research design needed to resolve the gap

A defensible follow-up should begin with community-approved, consent-based sampling of independent Su-pitha batches from named households/villages, recording the actual ingredient list and approximate proportions for each batch without publishing sensitive knowledge beyond agreed permissions. Preserve linked samples of raw cake, mature cake, mother-starter powder and fermentation stages. Record season, rice variety, cake age, drying/smoking/storage conditions and batch identifiers.

For laboratory work, use independent biological batches (not only technical replicates), appropriate negative/blank controls and validated methods. Characterise fungi/yeasts and bacteria with culture-dependent isolation plus ITS/16S or metagenomic methods; assay amylase/glucoamylase and relevant sugar profiles; quantify fermentation metabolites and ethanol in linked finished batches. If the community wishes to test individual plant contributions, use a separately approved, controlled design with ingredient-omission/add-back comparisons and sensory evaluation under appropriate ethical and safety review. Do not publish a reconstructed formula or claim causality from correlations alone. Benefit-sharing, attribution and control of community knowledge must be agreed before sampling or dissemination.

**Conclusion:** the current evidence supports a traditional multi-plant starter cake with six quantitatively prominent ingredients and an old-starter inoculation step. It does not support a taxon-by-taxon biochemical explanation. The microbiology of Ahom Xaj-pitha and Bodo emao supplies comparative hypotheses only. Direct Su-pitha sampling and linked microbial/enzymatic/chemical analysis remain the critical missing evidence.

## 24. Bichikri *Ardisia arborea*: historical Assam key and alternative-species comparison set (21 November 2026)

### 24.1 The 1939 *Flora of Assam* does not list *A. arborea*—but omission is not proof of absence

The preface is important: the authors explicitly say the flora was not fully comprehensive and omissions of trees, shrubs and herbs could not be avoided. Therefore the absence of *A. arborea* from this 1939 treatment is **not** evidence that the taxon could not occur in Assam. It does establish that the Bichikri name is not a straightforward match to one of the taxa treated in this historical regional key and that several alternative Assam *Ardisia* species must be considered.

### 24.2 The modern regional comparison set is broad

This is a discovery index, not a specimen record or the primary checklist itself; each candidate must be verified against primary taxonomic sources and voucher material.

The historical key and modern list together show why geographic mismatch alone cannot be used to “correct” the Bichikri plant. A locally plausible *Ardisia* may have been assigned the wrong species name, but the present evidence cannot select among alternatives. The NEHU Dhemaji *A. colorata* voucher (SRC-319) is one regional comparator, not a proposed replacement.

### 24.3 What would resolve the identification

A qualified Myrsinaceae/Primulaceae taxonomist should compare the original Bichikri material (if any) against the historical and current regional keys using, at minimum:

- leaf shape, size, margin, texture, gland dots and venation;
- indumentum on branchlets, petioles and leaf surfaces;
- inflorescence position and architecture (axillary/lateral versus terminal; umbels, racemes, corymbs or panicles);
- calyx and corolla size, shape and punctation;
- fruit shape, surface, colour and persistent floral structures;
- a complete collection label, locality, date, collector and accession.

Sterile photographs of a canopy or leaf alone may be insufficient for a secure determination. Any redetermination must be recorded separately from the source's printed *A. arborea* name.

## 25. Reproducibility audit of the Bichikri *Ardisia arborea* observation (21 November 2026)

### 25.1 What the published quantitative table actually supports

The original PDF of Baidya et al. (2020), SRC-316, has now been checked directly at the methods and table lines. The floristic survey ran for two years (2017–2018). The authors state that 10 m × 10 m tree quadrats (100 sampling units), 5 m × 5 m shrub quadrats (100 units) and 1 m × 1 m herb quadrats (200 units) were randomly laid. Tree girth at breast height was measured; species names were recorded with the help of caretaker knowledge, and samples of plants difficult to identify in the field were identified using *Flora of Assam* (Kanjilal & Bor 1938). The methods do not say that every tree was collected as a herbarium voucher, do not state that *A. arborea* was one of the difficult-to-identify samples, and do not give a specimen accession or determiner for this entry.

Table 1 places *Ardisia arborea* Koord. & Valeton under Primulaceae in the Bichikri tree layer and reports F = 2%, abundance = 1, density = 2 ha⁻¹, basal area = 0.09 m² ha⁻¹ and IVI = 3.33. Given the stated 100 tree sampling units, F = 2% is consistent with occurrence in two quadrats; abundance 1 and density 2 ha⁻¹ are internally consistent with two recorded individuals in the sampled tree layer, subject to the authors' unit conventions. This is a quantitative ecological record, not just a name in a narrative species list.

### 25.2 What cannot be reverse-engineered

The published table does not identify which two quadrats contained the plant, give the coordinates of either tree, provide individual girths, state whether the two records were separate stems or individuals under the authors' counting rule, or provide a photograph/collection number. The basal-area value and IVI cannot be used to reconstruct a unique tree identity or taxonomic diagnosis. Nor can the table establish whether the determination was made from sterile or fertile material.

The paper's general methods refer to samples for difficult field identifications, but the source does not connect any such sample to this species. It would be inaccurate to say categorically that the authors collected no vouchers; the precise conclusion is that **no voucher or specimen-level determination is reported for this particular table entry in the accessible article**.

### 25.3 A better, sharply scoped evidence request

The Tezpur University enquiry should request the following source records for this exact entry, rather than asking only whether the herbarium has the species name:

- Tree-quadrat field sheets for the 2017–2018 Bichikri survey, identifying the two *A. arborea* occurrences if the 2% frequency and abundance values represent two units/individuals.
- Tree numbers, quadrat IDs, plot layout and GPS coordinates or sketch maps.
- Individual girth-at-breast-height measurements used in the basal-area calculation.
- Field photographs showing leaves, branchlets, flowers/fruits and whole-tree habit.
- Any field collection, temporary sample number, herbarium accession, duplicate sheet or determination slip.
- The field assistant/caretaker who supplied the name and the botanist who confirmed or entered the identification.
- Any spreadsheet or calculation workbook used to generate Table 1, to check the two-occurrence interpretation and the rounding of density/basal area/IVI.

If those records do not exist, the publication still supports a Bichikri ecological occurrence report at the authors' stated identification level, but not a voucher-confirmed taxonomic range extension.

### 25.4 Evidence grade after direct methods review

**Grade: published quantitative field observation; species-level voucher unverified.** The table is stronger than an unreferenced secondary checklist because it reports a measured tree-layer occurrence with ecological parameters. It is weaker than a voucher-backed record because the paper does not link the name to a specimen, diagnostic image or determiner. Retain the printed identification and all reported metrics; annotate the specimen-level limitation. Do not silently downgrade the entire occurrence to “unsubstantiated,” and do not upgrade it to “confirmed Assam range extension.”

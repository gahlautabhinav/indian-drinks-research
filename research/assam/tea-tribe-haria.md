# Tea-tribe Haria — Assam

**Record:** `ASM-016-HAR`  
**Research status:** documented regional tradition; village-level attribution unresolved  
**Classification:** fermented rice-based alcoholic beverage (not recorded here as a distilled spirit)

## Summary

Haria is documented as a traditional rice-based fermented beverage associated with Tea-tribe / Adivasi tea-garden communities in southern Assam. A 2024 microbiome study includes Haria among five community beverages and reports a starter called **dawai**. A 2026 article describes a starter called **Dabai**, made in the account from rice and *Glycyrrhiza glabra* (Yastimadhu/licorice) bark. The spellings may be related, but the current evidence is not enough to assert that they are the same starter in all localities.

The 2026 article's study covered Cachar, Hailakandi and Karimganj (subsequently renamed Sribhumi). The accessible article text does not identify the precise Haria-sample villages. These districts therefore remain a regional study scope, not proof that every district or village has the same Haria tradition.

## Names and classification

- **Beverage name:** Haria.
- **Starter names reported:** Dabai (Yumnam, Nath & Sharma, 2026); dawai (Yumnam, Hazarika & Sharma, 2024).
- **Community label:** “Tea tribes” is a broad umbrella label for diverse Adivasi/tea-garden communities. Future research should identify the specific community, language and locality wherever the source allows.
- **Name collision warning:** Haria/Handia is also documented in West Bengal and east-central India. Do not assume all products with these names share a single recipe, starter culture, or cultural context.

## Ingredients and method reported in the sources

The 2026 account describes the following source-specific method:

1. Rice is soaked overnight and ground with the bark of *Glycyrrhiza glabra* (Yastimadhu/licorice).
2. The mixture is shaped into flat, rounded starter cakes and sun-dried; the account calls these cakes **Dabai**.
3. Rice is cooked and cooled, then mixed with the starter.
4. The mixture is kept covered in a clay pot for approximately 3–4 days.
5. The fermented mixture is strained and the liquid is consumed as Haria.

The 2024 paper calls the Tea-tribe starter **dawai** and describes fermentation periods broadly across its beverage set as variable, often several days to about a week. Do not combine these details into a single universal recipe until the naming and locality differences are resolved.

## Geography and fieldwork limits

The 2024 microbiome study reports 15 traditional beverage samples across five community cohorts, with three samples per cohort, including Tea-tribe Haria. Samples were collected before distillation. Its study region is southern Assam; the 2026 article describes fieldwork from May to October 2023 across Cachar, Hailakandi and Karimganj (renamed Sribhumi).

Neither the reviewed source passages provide a verified list of villages producing the Haria samples. No village names have been inferred for this record. A next step is to inspect supplementary sample metadata and cited Assam-specific Haria literature, then corroborate with community-authorized, locality-specific sources.

## Cultural context

The 2026 article describes Haria as a traditional community beverage and reports that consumption is especially prominent during summer. This is a source-specific statement, not a universal seasonal rule. Detailed ceremonial, life-cycle, gendered, or household practices should be added only with explicit source attribution and appropriate community consent.

## Evidence and cautions

- **Supported:** Haria is reported as a fermented rice-based beverage of Tea-tribe communities in southern Assam; starter names Dabai and dawai appear in two research sources; one article describes licorice bark in the Dabai starter and a 3–4-day fermentation.
- **Not yet established:** whether Dabai and dawai are identical terms; exact village and community identities of each sample; recipe variation across localities; current village-by-village production status.
- Microbiome findings describe sampled products and do not prove that Haria is clinically beneficial or microbiologically safe.
- Ethnomedicinal or cultural claims are not clinical evidence.
- Do not flatten the diverse Tea-tribe communities into one homogeneous tradition.

# SRC-071 — Saikia, Tag & Das (2007): Tai-Ahom Sajpani / Laopani full-text audit

**Audit date:** 20 November 2026  
**Citation:** Bhaskar Saikia, Hui Tag & A. K. Das (2007), “Ethnobotany of foods and beverages among the rural farmers of Tai Ahom of North Lakhimpur district, Asom,” *Indian Journal of Traditional Knowledge* 6(1):126–132.  
**Full text consulted:** https://www.academia.edu/48813140/Ethnobotany_of_foods_and_beverages_among_the_rural_farmers_of_Tai_Ahom_of_North_Lakhimpur_district_Asom  
**Official journal catalogue:** NIScPR Online Periodical Repository, IJTK Vol. 6(1), January 2007, pp. 126–132: https://nopr.niscpr.res.in/handle/123456789/732/simple-search?filtername=subject&filterquery=Ethnobotany&filtertype=equals

## 1. Study scope and provenance

The paper reports ethnobotanical fieldwork in rural Tai-Ahom-dominant areas of **North Lakhimpur district, Assam**, during **2005–2006**. Its methods state that oral and semi-structured questionnaire interviews were undertaken with **at least 15 local people**. Traditional rice varieties and local rice-beer preparation were observed and discussed on the spot; botanical information on plants used for beverage preparation was also collected.

The paper says plant material was identified using standard Indian Flora and deposited at the Herbarium of the Department of Botany, Rajiv Gandhi University, for future reference. It does not publish voucher accession numbers or identify the precise villages/households where each beverage or plant observation was made.

Do not interpret “at least 15” as an exact sample size. The paper is a historical field account, not a current production census.

## 2. Beverage identity and terminology

The paper explicitly calls the beverage:
- **Sajpani** in Tai
- **Laopani** in Assamese

It does not use Xajpani or Koloh pani in its beverage description. These labels should therefore remain source-specific. Sajpani/Laopani may be related to names used in other Ahom studies, but this paper alone does not prove exact synonymy with Xajpani, Haj pani or Koloh pani.

The paper calls the fermentation technique **Sewa diya**, and gives its Tai-language expression as **Lao fuk**. Preserve these process labels as printed pending language/community review.

## 3. Vekur pitha starter cake

The authors describe the traditional pitha as powdered rice grain mixed with wild plant material. Six plant taxa are named as ingredients:

| Printed name in SRC-071 | Plant part | Audit note |
|---|---|---|
| *Lygodium flaxuosum* Linn. | Leaves | Apparent spelling error; do not silently normalize |
| *Leucas aspera* Spreng. | Leaves | Botanical spelling as printed |
| *Cissampelos Pereira* Linn. | Leaves | Capitalization/taxonomic spelling as printed |
| *Scoparia dulsis* Linn. | Leaves | Apparent spelling error; likely intended spelling needs voucher check |
| *Cinamommum glanduliferum* Meissn. | Leaves | Apparent spelling error; verify against specimen |
| *Piper betle* Linn. | Leaves | Botanical spelling as printed |

The described preparation:
1. Collect leaves from the wild.
2. Sun-dry for 1–2 days.
3. Grind the dried leaves into powder.
4. Mix with powdered rice grain in a vessel with a small amount of water.
5. Add powder from previously prepared **Ghai pitha**, which the authors describe as a yeast source.
6. Roll the semi-solid mixture into plate/disc-shaped cakes.
7. Wrap the cakes in *Musa paradisiaca* leaves.
8. Keep them in an air-locked condition over fire heat for 4–5 days until dry.
9. Store the dried Vekur pitha for future use.

The authors report customary restrictions/taboos during starter preparation. These are source-specific cultural observations and should not be generalized to all Tai-Ahom households.

The article asserts that the starter contains *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* but does not describe a culture-based or molecular identification experiment in this paper. Treat this as the authors' reported statement, not as a laboratory-confirmed result from this study.

## 4. Rice and fermentation process

The paper identifies **Bora rice**, belonging to the Sali rice group, as the principal rice used in the beverage process described.

Its method:
1. Cook Bora rice.
2. Spread the cooked rice on a plain plate and leave it open for approximately one hour to cool and dry.
3. Mix the cooled rice with Vekur pitha.
4. Transfer the treated rice into a clay pot called **Kalah**.
5. Keep the pot in a dark place in a corner of the house/hut, in an air-locked condition.
6. Ferment for **4–5 days**.
7. Collect the concentrated alcoholic juice by filtration, using a vessel fitted to the mouth of the Kalah as a lid/filter arrangement or rice straw to prevent the semi-solid rice mass from escaping.

The article describes the juice as aromatic, sweet and alcoholic. It does not report an ethanol percentage, pH, microbial count, contaminant assay or formal safety testing.

## 5. Rice diversity reported

The study also documents local rice germplasm among Tai-Ahom farmers:
- 9 Ahu varieties
- 8 Sali varieties

It discusses traditional cultivation and grain characteristics. The rice-variety inventory is not a complete list of rice used in every household's beverage production. Bora is the rice explicitly associated with the beer method.

## 6. Cross-source comparison and ontology safeguards

### SRC-021 (Das & Deka 2012)
SRC-021 discusses Xaj pani/Koloh pani and Vekur pitha, with a separate survey context. Do not assume that the SRC-071 terms Sajpani/Laopani are exact synonyms without linguistic or community confirmation.

### SRC-314 (Borah et al. 2021)
SRC-314 Table 2 uses Haaj/Laupani and Haajpitha/Vekurpitha, and its Figure 3 caption calls the pictured beverage Xaajpani. It does not settle the terminology differences.

### SRC-315 (Bhuyan & Baishya 2013)
SRC-315 reports Haj/Haj pani/Koloh pani and Vekur pitha for a Baragharia, Golaghat account. This is a separate locality-specific source and should not be used to relocate SRC-071's North Lakhimpur observations.

### Archive treatment
- Keep the existing ASM-008-XAJ beverage record.
- Attach SRC-071 as a distinct primary-source evidence trail.
- Preserve Sajpani/Laopani as the names used by this paper.
- Do not add a second canonical beverage record until community/language evidence clarifies whether it is the same drink or a distinct preparation.
- Keep Ghai pitha (mother starter) distinct from Vekur pitha (prepared starter cake).

## 7. Limitations

1. No precise village or household list is given.
2. Interview sample is described only as “at least 15 local people”.
3. No voucher accession numbers are published in the paper.
4. Several plant names appear misspelled or inconsistently capitalized; taxonomic normalization requires the herbarium specimens or original field records.
5. No independent microbiological identification of the claimed yeast is described.
6. The process is one historical source-specific account, not a universal current recipe.
7. No finished-beverage chemistry, alcohol percentage, microbial safety, toxin or toxicology testing is reported.
8. Cultural and medicinal statements are ethnographic observations, not clinical efficacy.

**Conclusion:** SRC-071 is a primary ethnobotanical source with useful process detail: six named Vekur pitha plant taxa, Ghai pitha mother starter, Bora/Sali rice, Kalah earthen pot and 4–5 days of fermentation. Its most important archive contribution is not a new beverage record, but a source-specific Tai-Ahom name and method that should not be collapsed prematurely into the Xajpani/Haj/Koloh terminology used elsewhere.

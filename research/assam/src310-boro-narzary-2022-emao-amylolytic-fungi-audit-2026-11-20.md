# SRC-310 — Boro & Narzary (2022): Emao amylolytic fungi and laboratory rice-beer trials

**Audit date:** 20 November 2026  
**Citation:** Nitesh Boro and Diganta Narzary (2022), “Amylolytic Fungi in the Ethnic Beer Starter ‘emao’ and Their Beer-Producing Attributes,” *Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems* 6:869430. DOI: 10.3389/fsufs.2022.869430. Published 8 April 2022.  
**Full text:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sustainable-food-systems/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2022.869430/full  
**PDF:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sustainable-food-systems/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2022.869430/pdf

## 1. Sample provenance — precise correction

The Materials and Methods section says the traditionally prepared dried Emao sample was collected in a sterile container from a local brewer at **Rajapanichanda, Rani, Kamrup, Assam** and processed immediately for fungal culturing.

The authors also thank Ms. Deepa Kachari for providing Emao samples and information about brewing tradition.

A separate traditional rice beer called Jou, brewed by a local practitioner, was collected and used as the control in the laboratory fermentation comparison. The article does not give the control Jou's collection locality, brewer identity, batch date or production notes.

**Important cross-source discrepancy:** SRC-309 (Narzary et al. 2021) lists **Panichanda, Rani, Kamrup** as one of six Emao starter sample sites. SRC-310 uses **Rajapanichanda, Rani, Kamrup** for its single Emao starter sample. These strings may be related or may identify distinct places; the papers do not explicitly establish equivalence. Keep both source spellings until confirmed by authors, community informants or administrative records.

The 2022 paper does **not** say that its sample came from Boragari, Dotoma. Boragari, Dotoma is one of the 2021 SRC-309 sites. Do not transfer that location from the earlier study.

## 2. What was isolated

The authors homogenised 10 g of dried starter in 90 mL of 0.1% phosphate-buffered saline, made serial dilutions, and plated selected dilutions on Sabouraud dextrose agar containing tetracycline. Plates were incubated at 25 ± 1°C for 3–4 days. The average count from three replicate plates at the 10^-5 dilution was **13.53 × 10^7 CFU per gram** of Emao sample.

They randomly recovered 50 fungal isolates from mixed culture plates. The 50 were grouped into 11 morphology groups; one representative from each group was selected for ITS sequencing. The 11 representatives were assigned to 10 species, because Rhodosporidiobolus ruineniae was represented by two isolates:

| Isolate code | Assigned species | NCBI accession |
|---|---|---|
| Cgla_RF2 | Candida glabrata | OK073666 |
| Wano_RF3 | Wickerhamomyces anomalus | OK073667 |
| Rrui_RF4 | Rhodosporidiobolus ruineniae | OK073668 |
| Scer_RF6 | Saccharomyces cerevisiae | OK073669 |
| Sfib_RF11 | Saccharomycopsis fibuligera | OK073670 |
| Hbur_RF19 | Hyphopichia burtonii | OK073671 |
| Mind_RF25 | Mucor indicus | OK073672 |
| Pcit_RF32 | Penicillium citrinum | OK073673 |
| Cfab_RF37 | Cyberlindnera fabianii | OK073674 |

The paper's reported relative abundance values (W. anomalus 24%, C. glabrata 16%, H. burtonii 16%; several others 2%) are calculated among the **50 recovered culturable fungal isolates**. They are not direct relative-abundance measurements of the entire Emao microbiome. Culture media, incubation conditions and colony selection shape what can be recovered.

## 3. Enzyme activity

Five of the 11 representative isolates tested positive for amylase on starch agar:

| Isolate | Species | Amylase enzymatic index | Other reported activity |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Hbur_RF19 | H. burtonii | 2.09 ± 0.08 | Protease 1.20 ± 0.02 |
| Sfib_RF11 | S. fibuligera | 2.90 ± 0.02 | Protease 1.50 ± 0.04 |
| Mind_RF25 | M. indicus | 1.37 ± 0.04 | — |
| Mcir_RF48 | M. circinelloides | 1.21 ± 0.02 | — |
| Pcit_RF32 | P. citrinum | 1.68 ± 0.04 | Protease 1.60 ± 0.04; cellulase 1.24 ± 0.04 |

The remaining six representative isolates were negative for amylase in the plate assay. S. fibuligera had the highest reported amylase enzymatic index among the five positive strains. These are semi-quantitative plate-screening results, not direct enzyme yields in traditional brewing.

## 4. Ethanol production on glucose and soluble starch

Nine of the 11 representative isolates produced ethanol from glucose in the seven-day liquid assay. Five produced ethanol from soluble starch:

|---|---:|---:|
| Cgla_RF2 | 1.28 ± 0.03 | Not performed |
| Cfab_RF37 | 0.87 ± 0.03 | Not performed |
| Hbur_RF19 | 9.43 ± 0.07 | 8.924 ± 0.03 |
| Rrui_RF4 | No production | Not performed |
| Rrui_RF43 | No production | Not performed |
| Scer_RF6 | 11.45 ± 0.11 | Not performed |
| Sfib_RF11 | 6.16 ± 0.03 | 5.84 ± 0.04 |
| Wano_RF3 | 4.16 ± 0.03 | Not performed |
| Mind_RF25 | 2.78 ± 0.03 | 2.04 ± 0.03 |
| Mcir_RF48 | 8.05 ± 0.07 | 7.65 ± 0.06 |
| Pcit_RF32 | 7.17 ± 0.06 | 6.85 ± 0.03 |

The five starch-fermenting strains are the same five strains that tested amylase-positive. S. cerevisiae (Scer_RF6) produced the most ethanol from glucose, but it was amylase-negative and was not taken forward into the rice-starch beer trial. This illustrates the functional division between starch hydrolysis and ethanol production.

## 5. Pure-culture rice fermentation — not traditional Jou production

The five amylase-positive, starch-fermenting isolates were tested individually on a sterile rice substrate:

- 180 g dehusked glutinous rice (*Oryza sativa* var. *glutinosa*) per 500 mL borosilicate bottle.
- Rice was autoclaved at 15 lb pressure for 15 minutes.
- Each inoculum contained 2 g fresh-weight pure-culture fungal pellet suspended in 30 mL distilled water.
- Triplicate fermentation bottles per isolate.
- Incubation at 30 ± 1°C, loosely capped.
- Four isolates produced beer after 30 days; M. indicus Mind_RF25 required 180 days.

| Isolate | Fermentation duration | Ethanol (% v/v) | pH |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Hbur_RF19 | 30 days | 7.30 ± 0.09 | 3.41 ± 0.01 |
| Sfib_RF11 | 30 days | 4.55 ± 0.06 | 3.53 ± 0.01 |
| Mind_RF25 | 180 days | 3.17 ± 0.06 | 3.51 ± 0.017 |
| Mcir_RF48 | 30 days | 5.98 ± 0.03 | 3.70 ± 0.01 |
| Pcit_RF32 | 30 days | 5.65 ± 0.06 | 3.52 ± 0.01 |

The traditional Jou control had pH **3.78 ± 0.01** and ethanol **9.09 ± 0.03% v/v**. It was not a pure-culture experimental treatment; it was a traditionally brewed beverage collected from a local practitioner. The article does not provide the control's exact locality or batch metadata.

The 3.17–7.30% v/v range therefore describes the study's individual-strain laboratory fermentations, not the normal alcohol range of all Bodo Jou. The authors cite a separate source for traditional Jou at 12–19% v/v; that figure is not a result measured in this experiment.

## 6. Antibacterial and antioxidant assays — limits on interpretation

The authors extracted crude fungal metabolites and used agar-well diffusion against three laboratory test organisms: *Escherichia coli* MTCC 433, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* MTCC 2582 and *Staphylococcus aureus* MTCC 9886. Several isolates inhibited growth in these in-vitro plates; M. circinelloides, P. citrinum and H. burtonii were highlighted for activity against all three test organisms.

The beer samples were also assessed for ascorbic acid, total phenols, DPPH radical scavenging, total reducing power and total antioxidant activity. For example, Hbur_RF19 beer had the highest ascorbic acid (31.1 ± 1.27 mg/100 mL), total phenols (241.33 ± 2.30 mg/L), reducing power (0.396 ± 0.004 at A700) and total antioxidant activity (0.364 ± 0.001 g AAE/L) among the five pure-culture beers. Mcir_RF48 beer had the highest DPPH scavenging result (82.84 ± 0.27%).

**Do not translate these laboratory assays into a health or safety claim.** Inhibition zones against three test bacteria do not demonstrate pathogen elimination in a real beverage, do not establish safe production, and do not certify any fungal isolate for food use. The authors' discussion suggests antibacterial fungi could help maintain starter quality, but that is a research hypothesis, not a safety validation. The experiment did not test the complete microbial community, mycotoxins, contaminant loads, or consumer health outcomes.

## 7. Relationship to SRC-309 (2021)

| Dimension | SRC-309 (2021) | SRC-310 (2022) |
|---|---|---|
| Material | Six Emao starter samples | One Emao starter sample |
| Place as printed | Panichanda, Rani, Kamrup among six sites | Rajapanichanda, Rani, Kamrup |
| Method | Shotgun metagenomics on pooled six-sample material | Culture-dependent fungal isolation and strain-level functional tests |
| Resolution | Pooled community profile | Individual representative fungal isolates |
| Key contribution | 74 species and predicted functional potential | 11 representative isolates, enzyme tests and pure-culture rice fermentation |
| Main limit | Cannot attribute pooled reads to individual villages | One starter sample and culture-dependent selection |

These studies complement one another but should not be collapsed into one sample or one village record until the locality wording and sample provenance are confirmed.

## 9. Next research actions

1. Ask the authors whether Rajapanichanda and Panichanda refer to the same village/locality; preserve both until confirmed.
2. Request the exact village, date and batch provenance of the traditional Jou control.
3. Compare the 11 ITS accessions (OK073666–OK073676) against the 2021 pooled metagenomic results without assuming the two samples are identical.
4. Inspect the supplementary files for isolate-level measurements, assay replicates and any additional methodological details.
5. For any future food/industrial use of isolates, require strain-level safety assessment, toxin screening and controlled fermentation validation; do not infer food safety from this study's antibacterial assays.

**Conclusion:** SRC-310 provides the strongest strain-level functional evidence so far for fungi cultured from Bodo Emao. It demonstrates that some individual isolates can hydrolyse starch and produce ethanol in controlled rice fermentations. It does not establish that these isolates alone drive traditional Jou fermentation, nor that antibacterial activity makes the starter or finished drink safe.

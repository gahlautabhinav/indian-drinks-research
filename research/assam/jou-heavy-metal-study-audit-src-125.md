# Jou heavy-metal study audit — SRC-125

**Audit date:** 2026-10-28  
**Source:** Deka, Anamika Kalita; Handique, Pranami; Deka, Dibakar Chandra (2021). “Ethnic food beverages with heavy metal contents: Parameters for associated risk to human health, North-East India.” *Toxicology Reports* 8: 1220–1225. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.toxrep.2021.06.013  
**Open full text:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8233220/

## Research question

What can this analytical study establish about the three named Bodo Jou forms, and what should not be inferred from it for the Assam beverage archive?

## Sample design and scope

The authors report six beverage samples collected in Kokrajhar district: two samples each of Joubidwi (JB1–JB2), Joufinai (JF1–JF2) and Jougwran (JG1–JG2). Metals were measured using ICP-OES after acid digestion. Each sample was analysed in triplicate for reproducibility.

**Interpretation boundary:** triplicate measurements are analytical replicates, not three independent household batches. The paper reports only two samples per beverage type, from one district, and does not identify named villages or producers in the accessible methods. It is a small analytical case study, not a representative survey of Bodo production across Assam.

## Useful product-level evidence

- The article explicitly distinguishes Joubidwi as a fermented product, Joufinai as a preserved product, and Jougwran as a distilled product.
- It reports lower concentrations for several measured metals in the two Jougwran samples than in the undistilled Joubidwi/Joufinai samples and discusses distillation as a possible explanation.
- Under the authors’ chosen exposure assumptions, the reported total target hazard quotient (TTHQ) exceeded 1 for both Joufinai samples; Joubidwi and Jougwran sample TTHQ values were reported below 1 or close to 1.
- The paper's conclusion is conditional on its sample results, metal panel, reference doses, consumption assumption and calculation model. It is not a general certification that any Jou product is safe or unsafe.

## Data-quality and interpretation flags

1. **Very small, locality-limited sample:** six samples in total, only two per beverage type, all reported from Kokrajhar district. Do not generalise to all producers, villages, batches, seasons or registered GI products.
2. **Triplicates are not independent batches:** the methods say each sample was analysed in triplicate. This supports analytical repeatability only; it does not increase the biological/sample count beyond six.
3. **Reported mean ± SD entries require source-level checking:** several table entries display standard deviations implausibly large relative to their means (for example, JB1 cobalt is printed as 0.008 ± 1.01 mg/L; other entries show similar scale anomalies). Preserve the published table values as printed and seek author/source-data clarification before reusing uncertainty estimates.
4. **Table/narrative consistency needs review:** the narrative discusses some values as exceeding permissible limits while the paper's broad abstract/highlights summarise concentrations or intakes as within limits. The comparisons use multiple reference sources and limits, so do not compress them into a single “all metals safe” statement.
5. **Risk model assumptions are not measured consumption:** the THQ calculation assumes 250 mL/day and a specified exposure duration/body weight. This is a model scenario, not observed consumption by the sampled households.
6. **Units and reference limits need care:** the paper compares some beverage concentrations with drinking-water or wine-related limits. Such comparisons are contextual screens, not by themselves a beverage-specific regulatory compliance determination.
7. **No GI-specification equivalence:** the names Jougwran/Joubidwi in this paper do not establish that these sampled products meet the legal specifications for registered Bodo Jou Gwran or Bodo Maibra Jou Bidwi. The official GI Statements of Case and maps remain necessary.

## Archive decisions

- Record the article as a primary analytical source for the six Kokrajhar samples.
- Do not create village-level production rows because the article does not name sample villages.
- Do not change the canonical beverage names or merge product records based on this study alone.
- Do not use its conclusions as medical advice or as a current product safety guarantee.
- Keep the official GI specification gap ASM-GAP-026 open.

## Table-level reconciliation (2026-10-28)

The accessible full-text table and discussion do not support a simple blanket summary. The authors report Fe and Ni above the cited comparison values in all six samples and Mn above the cited value in Joubidwi/Joufinai; Table 5 reports TTHQ above 1 for both Joufinai samples. These are source-reported comparisons under the paper's model assumptions, not a beverage-specific regulatory determination or a diagnosis.

Table 1's accessible HTML renders unusually large SDs relative to several means, including JB1 Co 0.008 ± 1.01 mg/L, JB1 Cr 0.019 ± 0.40 mg/L, JF1 Cu 0.278 ± 2.11 mg/L and JG1 Cd 0.001 ± 1.10 mg/L. This may reflect reporting, typesetting or extraction problems; the HTML alone does not identify which. Do not silently repair values or treat these SDs as validated. The paper's abstract/highlights are broadly reassuring, while the detailed results include the comparison-limit exceedances and Joufinai TTHQ flags; preserve both and request source-data clarification before quantitative reuse.

The EDI/THQ calculation assumes 250 mL/day for a 60 kg adult. This is a model input, not a measured daily intake for each sampled source. The cited comparison values mix OIV wine-related and WHO drinking-water sources and should not be presented as a definitive Assam beverage-specific legal limit. Lower metal concentrations in the two Jougwran samples are a finding for those samples only, not proof that distillation universally removes contaminants or guarantees safety.

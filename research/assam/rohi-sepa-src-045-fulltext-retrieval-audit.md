## Claims supported by accessible metadata/abstract

- The paper treats Rohi and Sepa as two named traditional rice-based alcoholic beverages associated with Sonowal Kacharis.
- The abstract says both use a traditional fermentation process assisted by a starter called Mod Pitha, made using rice and multiple plants.
- The article's stated scope includes preparation, physicochemical parameters and antioxidant assays.
- The bibliographic record places the paper in *Indian Journal of Traditional Knowledge* 25(9), pages 914–921, September 2026.

These abstract-level claims do not establish that Rohi and Sepa have identical recipes, identical fermentation duration, identical final composition, or identical household practices.

## What remains unknown until the paper is read

1. Whether the methods describe separate preparation pathways for Rohi and Sepa, and what the paper calls each end product.
2. Sample count, household or batch provenance, district/village, collection dates and whether independent batches were used.
3. Ingredient and plant lists, taxonomic identifications, voucher specimens, plant parts, and whether each plant is explicitly linked to Mod Pitha or to a specific beverage.
4. Exact fermentation and storage conditions; equipment and any post-fermentation separation or distillation.
5. Physicochemical analytes, units, measurement methods, replicate design, uncertainty, and all numeric results.
6. Antioxidant assay type, controls, calculations and limitations. In-vitro antioxidant assay results are not evidence of clinical health benefits.
7. Whether the paper's terminology can resolve the existing question about how Sepa differs from Rohi or relates to other named Sonowal Kachari forms.

## Data-quality and interpretation controls

- Do not infer a specific village from the authors' affiliations or the statewide/community framing.
- Do not treat the abstract's shared Mod Pitha starter as proof that the two beverages have identical recipes.
- Do not add plant species to the canonical Mod Pitha ingredient list until the full methods/table support the exact taxon, plant part and source attribution.
- Do not infer health benefits from antioxidant assay claims.
- Do not use reported sample measurements as community-wide or species-wide reference ranges without reviewing sampling design and uncertainty.
- Keep Rohi and Sepa as separate named records pending source-level and community-preferred terminology comparison.

### Fieldwork and locality scope

The article states that four densely populated Sonowal Kachari villages—**Chaharikota, Teporguri, Bhoralibari and Meslou**—were visited between **June 2020 and March 2021**, 25–35 km from Dibrugarh University. Information was collected from aged and experienced villagers. The authors describe women as responsible for household preparation and state that some families sell their brew in local markets. The paper does not report the number of informants, households, producer households or beverage batches, nor does it map a specific Rohi/Sepa sample or plant inventory to each village. The acknowledgements name Khelanta Sonowal of Chaharikota village and Rajen Mili of Dhemaji district as information contributors; this does not supply a full sample frame. The authors state prior informed consent was obtained. Their generated dataset is available from the corresponding author on reasonable request and is not publicly accessible because of participant privacy and ethics considerations.

Four place rows ASM-PL-113–116 were added as **named field-study villages**, not village-level production censuses. They link to both canonical beverages only at the study-wide level; they do not claim that every village or household produces both fractions.

### Rohi and Sepa: extraction sequence resolved

The paper distinguishes the two products as sequential fractions of one fermented rice mass:

1. Half-cooked rice (called **Aaroi**) is cooled on a bamboo mat and mixed with powdered Mod pitha.
2. Water is added; the mixture is sealed in an aluminium **Handi** with banana leaves and left in a dark indoor corner.
3. Fermentation is reported as **4–5 days in summer** and **8–10 days in winter**.
4. The fermented mass is filtered through a bamboo basket (**Khorahi**) with perforated banana leaves. The first yellowish liquor is **Rohi**.
5. Water is added to the residual fermented mass, it is mixed, wrapped in clean cotton cloth and pressed. The whitish liquor is **Sepa**.

The article describes Rohi as the standard/superior class and Sepa as the regular/inferior class. Preserve this as an attributed local classification reported by the authors, not an objective quality judgement. The paper says Rohi may be stored in an earthen **Koloh** for about a year; it says Sepa does not keep as long and may become tangy, which the authors attribute to bacterial contamination. These storage statements are historical/source-specific reports, not food-safety advice.

### Starter-culture inventory

Table 1 contains **five entries, including rice**: *Oryza sativa* (Dhan; rice grain), *Casearia aculeata* (Khorkhoria pat; leaves), *Cinnamomum bejolghota* (Patihanda; “skins”), *Cyclosorus dentatus* (Saldaokunwi; leaves), and *Cinnamomum tamala* (Tezpat; leaves). The paper says the plant parts are washed and ground with pre-soaked rice in a wooden mortar and pestle, shaped into spheres, covered with *Cyclosorus dentatus* leaves and dried in a dark room for about two weeks; cakes are then wrapped in *Casearia aculeata* leaves, placed in a polythene bag and hung from the roof until use. Table 1 is transcribed in `rohi-sepa-mod-pitha-5-plants-src045.csv`.

The table cites references 18–20 for plant names/families/medicinal properties, and no voucher identifiers are reported. Do not treat these five taxa as a complete Assam-wide or all-village Mod pitha recipe. Medicinal-use statements in the table are traditional/source-reported claims, not clinical efficacy evidence. Taxonomic identity and plant-part terms require voucher/community reconciliation; preserve the published “skins” wording for *Cinnamomum bejolghota*.

### Laboratory validation and analytical data

The laboratory procedure used 1 kg sticky rice, approximately 10 g Mod pitha procured from Sonowal Kacharis, and 0.5 L distilled water. Rice was cooked in an aluminium Handi, cooled on a bamboo mat under banana leaf for two days, transferred to a plastic bucket, mixed with starter and water, and fermented at room temperature for four days. The authors say Rohi and Sepa were collected using the same sequential process described above.

Structured transcriptions were added:
- `rohi-sepa-physicochemical-src045.csv`: 29 long-format rows from Table 2 (four sample types and seven numeric/descriptive parameters, plus turbidity).
- `rohi-sepa-antioxidant-src045.csv`: 60 long-format rows from Tables 3–5 (storage-period TAA/TPPC/TFC, fresh-sample RSE/TAA/TPPC/TFC, and storage-period RSE).

The paper states determinations were done in triplicate and reports mean ± SD (n=3). It does not clearly establish whether these are independent biological batches, technical replicates, or the number of household samples. Treat the values as this study's reported measurements, not population norms. Table 4 includes home-brewed Rohi, home-brewed Sepa and lab-brewed Rohi, but no lab-brewed Sepa antioxidant row; the storage-time antioxidant tables focus on Rohi. This limits direct product-by-product comparisons. The authors report ethanol, pH, carbohydrate, protein, total solids, bitterness, colour and turbidity in Table 2; in-vitro antioxidant/phenolic measures are not evidence of clinical health benefits.

A caution on storage: the authors report visible “small microbes” after ten months and discuss increased antioxidant assay values during storage. This observation is not a microbiological safety assessment, and no safe storage duration should be inferred. Their community belief that medicinal value increases with storage is not clinically established.

### Research decision

The principal identity question is now substantially resolved: in this paper, Rohi is the first filtrate and Sepa is the pressed liquid from the residual mash. The remaining open questions are narrower: which village supplied each sample; whether both fractions are produced in every studied village/household; village-specific starter-plant lists; number and independence of household/batch samples; and access to the restricted underlying dataset. ASM-GAP-036 remains Open for those reasons, but no longer treats the Rohi/Sepa extraction distinction as unknown.

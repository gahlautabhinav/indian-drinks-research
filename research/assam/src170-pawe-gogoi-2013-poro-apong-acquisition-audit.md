## Field scope and methods

- The authors say ethnobotanical information was gathered in different Mishing-dominated localities of **Lakhimpur and Sonitpur districts**, Assam.
- The article names seven selected villages: **Dhunaguri, Badati, Bongalmora, Chamaguri Temona, Luitmukh, Kathalkuchi and Rajbari**.
- The text says village people who regularly prepare Poro Apong were interviewed and the complete preparation method was observed and documented.
- All plant species used for Apob cakes were collected and processed using standard herbarium methodology; identification began with standard literature and final confirmation was made at the Botanical Survey of India herbarium in Shillong (**ASSAM**) and Itanagar (**ARUN**).
- The article does not report field dates, respondent/informant count, selection criteria beyond regular Poro preparation, voucher accession numbers, or a village-by-taxon matrix. The seven listed villages are study sites, not evidence that each listed plant occurred in every village.

## Poro preparation account (source-specific)

The article describes two phases: Apob starter-cake preparation and Poro Apong brewing.

### Apob starter cake
- Materials named: rice (Saol), parts of herbs listed in Table 1, rice straw (Kher), bamboo tray (Dola), bamboo sieve (Salani), wooden grinder (Dheki or Khubuli), and a container (Charia).
- Plant parts were washed and placed in a Salani for 1–2 days to dry.
- 2–4 kg rice was soaked for about 2–3 hours, mixed with dried plant parts, ground, sieved and re-ground to obtain fine powder.
- Water was added to make a sticky paste; hand-formed cakes were reported as 5–7 cm diameter and 3–4 cm thick.
- Cakes were placed on straw on a Dola, covered with Bihalangani leaves (identified in the article as *Thelypteris parasitica* and *T. dentata*) and straw, then placed on a Dhuachang frame about 1 m above the kitchen fireplace.
- After 3–5 days the cakes were described as hard and ready for brewing.

### Poro brewing
- Materials named: rice (Saol), rice husk (Tuh), straw (Kher), banana leaf (Kolpat), bamboo net basket (Tum), iron pot (Kerahi), and large bamboo spoon (Heta or Durgi).
- Rice was washed and cooked; when half-cooked, straw and rice husk were mixed and fired to make ash.
- Cooked rice was cooled on a Dola, mixed with the ash, then 4–5 crushed Apob cakes were added.
- The mixture was tightly packed with banana leaves in a bamboo basket and stored for fermentation.
- The authors report **7–12 days** depending on season, sometimes more than a month.
- The fermented mixture was filtered with water through a **Tasuk**, a V-shaped bamboo utensil lined with banana leaves and straw; re-filtering continued until the liquid colour changed from black to brown. The resulting liquid is identified as Poro Apong / Chai Mod.

This article's account describes fermentation followed by water filtration through Tasuk. It does **not** describe distillation in the recovered method. Do not generalize its times, ratios or equipment to all Mising households or reconcile other studies' differing descriptions without further evidence.

## Cultural and health-claim caution

The article describes Poro Apong as important at religious festivals and ceremonies. It also repeats traditional medicinal/health claims, including urinary-problem claims and advice about limited consumption. These are claims reported by the authors/community, not clinical efficacy, toxicology, alcohol-risk or food-safety evidence. They must not be presented as medical advice.

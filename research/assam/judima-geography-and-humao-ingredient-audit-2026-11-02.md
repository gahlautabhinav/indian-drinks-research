**Scope:** Reassess ASM-GAP-001 (village/block distribution) and ASM-GAP-002 (Humao/Umhu ingredients and preparation variation) using full-text review of SRC-001, SRC-002 and SRC-003 and the official GI registry SRC-004.

### GI geography is not a village production census

The official IP India registry lists Judima application 643 as registered, applicant address at Dibarai, and geographical area as Assam. It also lists separate documents for the map, producer list and Statement of Case. The registry's applicant address is not evidence that every household in Dibarai produces Judima; the broad geographical-area field is not evidence that every Assam district has current producers. The underlying map, producer list and Statement of Case were not extractable from the registry page during this pass. Therefore the GI geography question remains open pending primary-document retrieval.

## 2. Humao ingredient and method comparison

### SRC-001 — Dhansiri, 2022

- Humao starter cake: rice plus bark of Thempra, identified by the article as *Acacia pennata*.
- Bark is sun-dried, cut and powdered; soaked rice is ground with a traditional mortar (Rimin), mixed with bark powder and a little water to form dough.
- Cakes are described as roughly 5–7 cm radius and 80–100 g; old rice cake is sprinkled over new cakes.
- Cakes are sun-dried for 4–5 days and stored cool.
- For the beverage, Bairing or Bora rice is preferred; cooked rice is cooled on bamboo mats, mixed with Humao, and held in earthen or bamboo vessels called khulu under jute gunny bags.
- Slightly yellow liquid appears after 5–7 days; the source notes that summer fermentation is faster. The liquid may be collected, strained and diluted.

**Scope:** One Dhansiri field study and a documented producer household. The article does not establish a universal Dimasa recipe.

### SRC-002 — Dibarai, survey in 2019

- The article's preparation diagram identifies bark of Thembra as *Acacia pennata* and describes rice powder plus bark as the Humao ingredients.
- The diagram gives a rice-to-cake ratio of 1 kg rice to 12–15 g cake and depicts yellow juice emerging after 24 hours.
- The article's diagram is presented as adapted from earlier work; its 24-hour figure should not be silently combined with the 5–7-day fermentation account in SRC-001 as if they were the same measured protocol.
- The Dibarai survey itself was about cultural association with Judima and does not constitute a representative recipe survey.

**Scope:** Diagram-level preparation lead, not a universal fermentation schedule or village prevalence finding.

### SRC-003 — Tereh, household case in December 2022

- Khimkho Hojai describes using maisha (white rice), maiju (red rice) and baireng (a bitter variety used for brewing); she uses maiju and baireng in the observed household practice.
- The article describes mature Thembra bark (reported as over seven years old) and a sweet bark preference for the starter.
- Rice powder and bark are made into Humao; the account documents ritual precautions, a prayer and separation of sour foods from the brewing area.
- Brewing is described as a year-round activity in this household, with post-harvest brewing for Bushu. The report notes monsoon moisture and summer temperature effects on fermentation.
- Surangdisa is mentioned as a location where a Thembra vine was replanted. The article does not document Judima production there; do not add Surangdisa as a beverage-production locality on this basis.

**Scope:** One household's knowledge and practice, not a universal recipe or a representative sample.

## 3. Cross-source conclusions

1. *Acacia pennata* bark, named Thembra/Thempra in the sources, is a shared starter ingredient across the three accounts. Botanical voucher confirmation and community-led naming remain desirable.
2. Rice varieties differ by source and household: Bairing/Bora in SRC-001; the SRC-003 household describes maisha, maiju and baireng, and specifically uses maiju plus baireng.
3. Starter proportions, cake size, drying time, vessel names, extraction timing and fermentation duration are not identical across the sources. Preserve them as source-specific observations.
4. The SRC-002 24-hour diagram and SRC-001 5–7-day process must not be collapsed into a single universal schedule.
5. No evidence supports one standard Humao recipe for every Dimasa locality.

## 4. Repository changes

- Updated SRC-001 to record Dhansiri methods and its one-household limitation.
- Updated SRC-002 to record the Dibarai survey design and its limited producer attribution.
- Updated SRC-003 to record the Tereh household field case and Surangdisa caveat.
- Refined ASM-PL-001, ASM-PL-002 and ASM-PL-003 to distinguish evidence tiers.
- Updated ASM-GAP-001 and ASM-GAP-002 with the full-text findings and next steps.
- Both gaps remain **Open**: geographic distribution is not exhaustive, and locality-specific recipe variation still needs broader community validation.

## 5. Next evidence needed

- Obtain the official Judima GI map, Statement of Case and producer list from IP India or the applicant; transcribe the geography without assuming that registration area equals active production.
- Seek community-confirmed village and block coverage across Dima Hasao and any other claimed Dimasa production areas.
- Verify Thembra/Thempra identity with a botanical voucher and Dimasa knowledge holders.
- Compare recipes only with explicit locality, household, date and consent context; do not publish restricted ritual knowledge without permission.

**Conclusion:** Three named Dima Hasao localities now have differentiated evidence in the archive. This improves locality confidence but does not close the geographic-distribution gap or establish a universal Humao recipe.

### Secondary geographic lead: CII 2024 GI booklet (SRC-130)

The Confederation of Indian Industry's 2024 *A Booklet on Geographical Indications of Northeast States of India* (page 74; printed page numbering) states that Judima is produced by Dimasa communities residing mostly in **Dima Hasao and Karbi Anglong districts**. It repeats the applicant address at Dibarai and summarizes Humao/Thembra-based production.

This adds a secondary district-scope statement beyond the IP India registry's broad “Assam” field. It is not the official GI map, does not provide a village-level producer list, and does not prove current production in every part of either district. Karbi Anglong already has independent literature/locality leads in this archive (including Kathkatia/Silonijan and Diphu sample collection); the CII statement is retained as district-level corroboration only, not a new village record.

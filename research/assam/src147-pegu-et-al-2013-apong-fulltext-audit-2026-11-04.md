# SRC-147 — Pegu et al. (2013), Mising Apong cultural and process account

**Audit date:** 2026-11-04  
**Article:** Rajeswar Pegu, Jitu Gogoi, Ajit K. Tamuli & Robindra Teron (2013), “Apong, an Alcoholic Beverage of Cultural Significance of the Mising Community of Northeast India,” *Global Journal of Interdisciplinary Social Sciences* 2(6):12–17, ISSN 2319-8834.  
**Full text:** https://gifre.org/admin/papers/gjiss/ALCOHOLIC%20-vol-2-6-gjiss.pdf

## 1. Study scope and method

- The paper says fieldwork was undertaken in 2010 and 2012 among Mising communities in Dhemaji district and Majuli Island (Assam), and among Mising/Miri communities at Pasighat and Oyan in East Siang district (Arunachal Pradesh).
- It describes participatory methods: informal/group discussions, semi-structured interviews with key informants, personal observation, attendance at rituals/festivals and interviews with women and priests.
- Plant material used in E’pob was recorded using Mising local names, collected with local guides, identified using Kanjilal and Bor (1934–40), and processed into voucher specimens using standard methods.
- The paper does not provide the number of informants, sample allocation by locality, voucher accession numbers or a village-by-village dataset. Do not invent those details or create precise village records.
- The paper's broad geography includes two Assam locations and two East Siang localities in Arunachal Pradesh. Only Assam evidence belongs in this Assam archive; the Arunachal material should be retained as a cross-state lead rather than imported as Assam locality evidence.

## 2. E’pob starter-cake account

- Rice grain is the major starter component, mixed with leaves of plant species.
- Leaves are cleaned and dried in sun or on a bamboo platform over the fireplace (Pe’rap/Rabbong); the paper gives 10–15 days depending on weather.
- Dried leaves are pounded in a wooden mortar; soaked rice is mixed with the leaf powder and pounded with water until a sticky paste forms.
- A quantity of good-quality old E’pob is added as microbial spawn.
- Cakes are made in multiple forms, including round/egg-like/flat cakes, around 6 × 3 cm; placed on paddy straw and Rukji leaves (*Cyclosorus extensa*, spelling as printed), covered with another layer and dried 3–5 days depending on ambient temperature.
- Cakes are stored, preferably in earthen pots, covered with Rukji leaves and kept near a fireplace.
- The paper says that formerly more than 50 plants were reportedly used and that about 26 plants were used “today” at the time of the study. It does not supply a complete 26-entry inventory table in the accessible article text. Treat these as author-reported aggregate counts, not a transcribed species list or present-day prevalence estimate.
- The article mentions a few plants in its origin narrative (including Rukji, Bungkiripuk, Rikom and several aromatic/taste plants), but that mythic narrative is not a systematic starter inventory.

## 3. Two distinct Apong processes

### Po:ro Apong / Saimod

- Paddy husk and dried paddy straw are partially burned and the ash collected.
- Cooked rice is cooled on banana leaf and mixed with the ash; powdered E’pob is added.
- The article reports a general ratio of one E’pob cake to 1 kg rice grains.
- Fermentation occurs in a fumigated earthen Kili’ng, closed with dried straw and Rukji leaves.
- Time is reported as 6–8 days in warm weather and 12–15 days in cold weather.
- Extraction uses a cone-shaped bamboo Ta:suk lined with banana leaves and a paddy-straw pack at the base. Fermented rice is placed inside and water poured through it; the filtrate is collected as Apong.
- The account says water is added 3–4 times for extraction. The residual Po:ro tonrug is reported as pig feed or fish bait.
- The paper records straw-coloured product and sometimes reddish-black appearance. This is a descriptive report, not a quality specification.

### Nogin Apong

- Cooked rice is cooled, worked to fine grains and mixed with powdered E’pob.
- Fermentation is in an earthen pot covered with paddy straw and Rukji leaves.
- Time is reported as 5–6 days in warm weather and 8–10 days in cold weather.
- Extraction is described separately from Po:ro: fermented Nogin rice is mixed/stirred with water, then passed through a woven split-bamboo Korai sieve. The filtrate is collected as Nogin Apong; residual substrate is called Nogin tonrug.
- No ash addition or Ta:suk is described for Nogin in this account.

## 4. Comparison with other primary accounts

| Source | Nogin fermentation | Po:ro fermentation | Extraction |
|---|---|---|---|
| SRC-147 Pegu et al. (2013) | 5–6 days warm; 8–10 cold | 6–8 warm; 12–15 cold | Nogin: stirred with water and Korai; Po:ro: repeated water percolation through Ta:suk |
| SRC-145 Handique & Deka (2016) | 4–5 days summer; 8–9 winter | No Po:ro method in Mising subsection | Cloth filtration after water addition |
| SRC-084 Borah (2026) | 10–15 days summer | 10–15 days summer | Nogin: water poured over rice; Po:ro: Ta:suk |
| SRC-136 Kardong et al. (2012) | Not the focus | About 20 days at 30–35°C | Ta:suk |

These are source-specific observations and reports. Differences may reflect locality, season, substrate, starter amount, vessels, reporting conventions or household practice. Do not average them into one universal fermentation period. SRC-145's cloth filtration is specifically labelled Nogin in that paper; it should not be reassigned to Po:ro.

## 5. Cultural and health-claim boundary

- The paper discusses the different ritual/social contexts of Nogin and Po:ro and documents women’s role in starter preparation and beverage production, with a stated exception for extraction during one ritual context.
- It records a local belief that Po:ro Apong consumption may prevent kidney stones, while the paper itself says this needs investigation. This is a reported folk belief, not medical evidence or a recommendation.
- It describes Nogin as locally claimed to be nutritious/energizing; this is not a measured nutritional result in this paper.
- The article explicitly notes further research is needed on microbial flora, fermentation dynamics, metabolites, nutrients, toxicity and preservation.

## 6. Archive decisions

1. Register as a distinct primary qualitative/cultural and process source (SRC-147); do not confuse it with Pawe & Gogoi (2013), which remains SRC-135 and concerns ethnobotany of Poro Apong.
2. No new beverage rows are required: Nogin and Po:ro Apong are already represented.
3. No village-level place rows are justified from this source; its Assam geography is district/island-level and field allocation is unspecified.
4. Do not create a 26-plant CSV: no complete inventory is printed in the accessible article.
5. Preserve the source's separate Nogin and Po:ro processes, seasonal time ranges, and different extraction devices.
6. Keep the folk kidney-stone claim clearly labelled as an unverified traditional belief.

**Conclusion:** SRC-147 provides an independent, full-text 2013 process and cultural account. It strengthens the distinction between Nogin and ash-added Po:ro Apong and gives separate seasonal fermentation and extraction descriptions, but does not provide a complete starter-plant inventory or village-level sampling data.

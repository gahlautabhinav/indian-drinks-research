# SRC-343/SRC-344 — Judima chapter experimental-data provenance audit and Boro Jou comparison

**Audit date:** 25 November 2026

This audit evaluates Ron Kemprai's chapter in *Studies on Dimasa History, Language and Culture*, Vol. 1 (2017), against two independently accessible 2014 papers: Arjun, Verma & Prasad's Judima study and Basumatary et al.'s Boro Jou biochemical study.

## SRC-343 — Kemprai (2017), “Physico-chemical and Biochemical Properties of Judima (Rice Beer)”

**Citation:** Ron Kemprai (2017), “Physico-chemical and Biochemical Properties of Judima (Rice Beer),” in Monali Longmailai (ed.), *Studies on Dimasa History, Language and Culture*, Vol. 1, DVS Publishers, Guwahati, pp. 170–179. ISBN 978-93-85839-08-5.  
**Accessible full text:** https://www.academia.edu/38406116/Studies_on_Dimasa_History_Language_and_Culture_Volume_1

### What the chapter says it did

- It describes Judima as a Dimasa homemade rice beverage.
- It attributes the recipe information to “local Dimasa women of district” but does not name the district, villages, number of informants, household count, dates or sampling design.
- It says the experimental fermentation was performed in laboratory conditions using rice identified as *Oryza* sp. var. “Maiju” and Humao starter in a sterilized medium.
- It says samples were harvested over a period ending at 24 days and biochemical analysis was performed in triplicate. The harvest schedule is internally unclear: the text says “total 3–4 times,” first on day 4, then on 1st, 2nd and 4th day, then every fourth day to 24 days; the results also describe a day-0 sample as harvested the day after inoculation. The number and timing of actual harvests cannot be reconstructed confidently from the prose.
- Methods listed include pH, total solids, qualitative and quantitative carbohydrate, reducing sugar, protein, free amino acids, total/volatile acidity and alcohol by dichromate oxidation/titration.

### Values reported by Kemprai (2017)

| Measure | Value as printed |
|---|---:|
| pH | 4.21 ± 0.04 |
| Total solids | 0.370 ± 0.03 |
| Carbohydrate | 48 ± 3.02 mg/ml |
| Reducing sugar | 3.47 ± 0.24 mg/ml |
| Protein | 1.26 ± 0.10 mg/ml |
| Free amino acids | 3.1 ± 0.18 mg/ml |
| Total acidity | 1.48% ± 0.24 |
| Volatile acidity | 1.18% ± 0.19 |
| Alcohol | 18.53 ± 6.17% v/v; stated range 5.30–22.05% v/v |

The chapter reports light golden-brown colour, transparent liquid and sweet/tongue-sensitising taste. These are source-reported observations, not standardised sensory-panel results.

## SRC-344 — Basumatary et al. (2014), “Biochemical analysis of Jou”

**Citation:** Tarun Kumar Basumatary, Reena Terangpi Basumatary, Subhash Medhi, Sujoy Bose and Ramie Husneara Begum (2014), “Biochemical analysis of Jou: a traditional drink of the Boro tribe of Assam and North East India,” *IOSR Journal of Environmental Science, Toxicology and Food Technology* 8(7), Version I, pp. 99–103.  
**Full text:** https://www.academia.edu/105608297/Biochemical_analysis_of_Jou_a_traditional_drink_of_the_Boro_tribe_of_Assam_and_North_East_India

### What the Boro Jou paper says it did

- Its study is explicitly in Kokrajhar district and concerns Boro women/informants.
- It says preparation information came from local Boro women; laboratory fermentation used *Oryza sativa* var. Ranjit and Amao starter in an earthen pot.
- It reports seven harvests: day 0, 1, 2 and 6, then every sixth day through day 24.
- It describes essentially the same analytical methods and reports a biochemical table with values that match Kemprai's Judima chapter.

### Exact numerical overlap and textual similarity

The following figures are identical between Kemprai (2017) Judima chapter and Basumatary et al. (2014) Boro Jou paper:

- pH 4.21 ± 0.04;
- carbohydrate 48 ± 3.02 mg/ml;
- reducing sugar 3.47 ± 0.24 mg/ml;
- protein 1.26 ± 0.10 mg/ml;
- free amino acids 3.1 ± 0.18 mg/ml;
- total acidity 1.48% ± 0.24;
- volatile acidity 1.18% ± 0.19;
- alcohol 18.53 ± 6.17% v/v;
- alcohol range 5.30–22.05% v/v;
- total solids 0.370 ± 0.03 (the Jou article's table prints this value, although its abstract prints 0.306 ± 0.03).

There is also substantial phrase-level and sequence-level similarity in the results discussion: the same progression from pH, acidity, macronutrient values, fermentation-age trends and alcohol range appears in both texts. The chapter's harvest schedule is not the same as the Jou paper's schedule, and the substrate/starter/community are different (Maiju/Humao/Dimasa versus Ranjit/Amao/Boro).

### Integrity assessment

This is a material data-provenance anomaly. The Judima chapter reports a set of values and results narrative that exactly match the earlier Boro Jou paper, despite describing a different community, rice variety, starter culture and harvest schedule. The chapter does not cite Basumatary et al. (2014) in its short reference list. The chapter's method and sample schedule are also internally inconsistent.

The evidence supports flagging **possible unacknowledged reuse/adaptation of the Jou dataset or text**, but it does not establish intent, authorship misconduct or the actual laboratory provenance. Only original lab notebooks, sample records, raw data, correspondence or a transparent author clarification could resolve that. Do not make a plagiarism allegation as a settled fact.

**Archive rule:** Do not treat Kemprai's 2017 numerical values as an independent, validated Judima measurement series. Keep the chapter as a source that reports those values, but mark the values as provenance-disputed / not independently attributable to Judima until primary data are available. Do not use its 18.53% mean or 22.05% maximum as a Judima benchmark, GI specification, product standard or safety claim.

## Comparison with the independent Judima study — Arjun, Verma & Prasad (2014)

**Citation:** Jashodeb Arjun, A. K. Verma and S. B. Prasad (2014), “Method of preparation and biochemical analysis of local tribal wine Judima: an indigenous alcohol used by Dimasa tribe of North Cachar Hills District of Assam, India,” *International Food Research Journal* 21(2), pp. 463–470.  
**Full text:** https://www.academia.edu/76485333/Method_of_preparation_and_biochemical_analysis_of_local_tribal_wine_Judima_an_indegenous_alcohol_used_by_Dimasa_tribe_of_Assam_India

This paper provides a separate Judima field and laboratory study:
- Field survey: 100 participants (79 female, 21 male), ages 30–60; semi-structured questionnaire, visits to homes, workplaces and wine-selling markets; local-language face-to-face interviews; consent reported.
- It says the traditional method was observed and documented in North Cachar Hills district.
- It describes rice cakes using glutinous rice and *Acacia pennata* bark; starter cake called Umhu/Humao; other plant parts named include *Piper betle*, *Buddleja asiatica* and *Hedyotis scandens*.
- Preparation section says one large Humao cake is sufficient for 5 kg rice; fermented mass is covered with jute gunny bags; after 5–7 days yellowish juice appears, then diluted/filtered for consumption.
- The table reports alcohol 16 ± 2% v/v, carbohydrate 32.43 ± 2.7 mg/ml, protein 0.97 ± 0.18 mg/ml, free amino acids 3.21 ± 0.21 mg/ml, pH 5.1 and other values.
- The article reports voucher specimens JA-1 to JA-4 deposited in the Department of Zoology, Lumding College, for four plant materials.

This is not automatically the definitive Judima specification either: it is one study and its recipe/measurements should remain attributed to its sample and field frame. Its field study is nevertheless independent and more explicitly documented than Kemprai's chapter in sample design and preparation description.

## Further internal and methodological cautions

1. Kemprai's introduction says biochemical analysis of Judima had not been documented, although it cites Arjun et al. (2014) in the chapter's references. This is an internal literature-review inconsistency.
2. Kemprai's chapter reports a 24-day series, but its prose about harvest frequency/timing does not reconcile with the day-0-to-day-24 results.
3. The chapter does not disclose the number of independent batches, exact sample counts, district, village, informant count, rice-to-starter ratio, starter preparation steps or full lab replication design.
4. The alcohol assay is described only as dichromate oxidation followed by titration against sodium thiosulfate; the chapter does not provide enough detail to assess validation, calibration, recovery or uncertainty.
5. The chapter's conclusion says household manufacture can involve poor hygiene and short shelf life, but it gives no measured microbial load, pathogen screen, shelf-life trial or contaminant analysis in the chapter.
6. The chapter's suggestions about possible therapeutic phytochemicals are hypotheses, not measured clinical benefits. No human health outcome or finished-product toxicology was tested.
7. The phrase “biochemical enrichment” and presence of macronutrients do not imply that alcoholic Judima is a health supplement or safe at any intake level.

## Archive decisions

1. Register Kemprai (2017) as SRC-343 and Basumatary et al. (2014) as SRC-344.
2. Keep Arjun et al. (2014) as the independent Judima field/lab comparator already registered elsewhere in the archive.
3. Record the exact overlap as a serious data-provenance anomaly, with cautious wording (“possible unacknowledged reuse/adaptation”) rather than a settled misconduct allegation.
4. Exclude Kemprai's 18.53% mean and 22.05% maximum from Judima product benchmarks and any district-wide range until the underlying data are independently verified.
5. Keep Arjun et al.'s 16 ± 2% as a separate source-attributed experimental result, not a universal value.
6. Do not create a village-level production record from Kemprai's vague “local Dimasa women of district.”
7. Do not infer product safety, nutritional benefit or clinical efficacy from the assays described.

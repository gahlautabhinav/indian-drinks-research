# Tai-Ahom Xajpani / Xaj pani / Koloh pani — Assam

**Archive ID:** ASM-008-XAJ  
**Status:** Core identity documented; terminology and current locality-level production remain provisional.  
**Community:** Tai-Ahom / Ahom  
**Type:** Fermented rice-based alcoholic beverage; the sources reviewed do not establish the core form as distilled.

## Naming and classification

The 2012 survey by Das, Deka and Miyaji calls the beverage **Xaj pani or Koloh pani** and places its study in Sivasagar district. Bhuyan and Baishya (2013) use **Haj pani** for the Ahom beverage and also refer to Koloh pani. Other reviews use **Xaj / Xaj-pani** and mention **Haaj or Laopani**. These names overlap in the literature, but the archive does not assume every term is a perfect synonym. Confirm preferred spelling, pronunciation and distinctions with Tai-Ahom language and community sources.

Starter terminology is separate from beverage terminology. Sources report **Vekur pitha**, **Xaj-pitha / Xajor pitha**, and **Haaj-pitha**. **Ghai pitha** is described in a 2023 review as a parent/previous starter used to seed a new starter. Do not treat these starter names as beverage names.

## Ingredients and starter tradition

The 2012 survey reports rice, water and plant materials used in one starter-cake tradition. Its Sivasagar section lists leaves of *Oldenlandia corymbosa*, *Lygodium* sp., *Hydrocotyle sibthorpioides*, *Centella asiatica* and *Cissampelos pareira*, and seeds of *Piper nigrum*. These are one source-specific inventory, not a universal Tai-Ahom recipe. A 2023 ethnobotanical survey reports a larger Sivasagar inventory; different counts across studies should remain separate until methods and locality coverage are reconciled.

## Published process descriptions

Das, Deka and Miyaji (2012) describe selected plant parts being cleaned/dried and ground with rice and a little water to form oval cakes, which are dried before use. Their beverage description uses partly cooked rice cooled on banana leaves, mixed with powdered starter and placed in a sealed earthen vessel; after a reported 3–5 days, water is added and the mass is strained. This is a historical, source-specific account, not a universal production instruction.

Bora et al. (2016) describe a liquid called **rohi** collected during the fermentation setup, with the liquid decanted and diluted to serve as **xaj**. The exact relation among rohi, xaj and xajpani should be recorded as described by each source rather than harmonised prematurely. Do not infer that every household follows the same sequence or uses the same vessel arrangement.

## Cultural context

Reviews associate Xaj with Tai-Ahom social and religious life and mention Bihu. Duarah, Kalita and Mishra (2023) report starter preparation around Rongali and Bhogali Bihu and a community function called Haaj’r hokam. These are source-attributed leads, not assumptions about every household or every present-day locality. Current practices and which details are public should be verified with community-led sources and consent-based fieldwork.

## Geographic evidence

- **Sivasagar district:** the 2012 preparation survey's Xaj section.
- **Titabar subdivision, Jorhat district:** Bora et al. (2016) collected Xaj-pitha from five rural Ahom households in August 2014.
- **Six-district starter sampling:** Keot et al. (2020) sampled Xaj-pitha across 12 clusters in Tinsukia, Dibrugarh, Sivasagar, Jorhat, Lakhimpur and Dhemaji. Its table names Margherita and Sadiya (Tinsukia); Moran and Naharkatiya (Dibrugarh); Bokota and Sonari (Sivasagar); Titabar and Nimati (Jorhat); Bihpuria and Dhakuakhana (Lakhimpur); Bordoloni and Silapathar (Dhemaji). These are starter-sampling localities, not a census of current beverage production.
- **Baragharia village, Golaghat:** Bhuyan and Baishya (2013) identify this as the study locality for their Ahom/Haj section, which describes Haj pani/Koloh pani and Vekur pitha. Treat it as a historical locality lead, not proof of current production.
- **Luk-Lau / Nam-Lau / Lao-pani:** Assam Tourism lists Luk-Lau for Ahoms; a 2015 cultural overview also lists Luk-Lau, Nam-Lau and Lao-pani. These names remain unverified in relation to Xajpani/Koloh pani/Haj pani; do not merge them without Tai-Ahom language or community confirmation.

## Microbiology and safety boundary

Bora et al. (2016) examined microbial communities associated with Xaj-pitha. Keot et al. (2020) assessed microbial quality and potential contaminants in sampled starter cakes. These findings apply to the samples and methods reported; they do not establish the microbial profile or safety of every household batch. Traditional or ethnobotanical medicinal claims are not clinical evidence. Reported fermentation durations must not be treated as food-safety guarantees.

## Research gaps

1. Confirm Tai-Ahom-preferred names and whether Xajpani, Xaj pani, Koloh pani, Haj pani, Haaj and Laopani are synonyms, regional variants or partly distinct preparations.
2. Verify current beverage production village by village. Treat the six-district 2020 study as starter-sampling evidence, not proof of current production in every locality.
3. Compare starter-cake names and plant inventories across localities without merging distinct recipes or knowledge systems.
4. Verify contemporary Bihu and ritual contexts through community-led sources, respecting boundaries around restricted knowledge.
5. Recover the primary source underlying the Baragharia, Golaghat locality reference.

## Archival evidence for Luk Lau

A British Library Endangered Archives Programme catalogue record for the Tai-language manuscript **Khon Ming** (catalogue dating: 18th century) describes a *Dam Phi* mantra inviting deities to receive **Luk Lau**, Phek Khu and Phak Tai annually, and on births, marriages and death anniversaries. This is stronger historical evidence that the term Luk Lau belonged to a ritual offering context than the modern tourism name-list alone. It still does **not** tell us the ingredients or preparation of Luk Lau, nor establish that Luk Lau is identical to Xajpani/Koloh pani/Haj pani. The catalogue summary is not a full critical edition or a village-level field record. [SRC-058](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-003345056) The catalogue states that digitised content is available at [EAP373/4/25](https://eap.bl.uk/archive-file/EAP373-4-25) (8 TIFF images); the manuscript images need reading by a qualified Tai/Ahom reader before stronger linguistic or product claims are made.

Research consequence: keep Luk-Lau as a historically grounded naming lead and cultural-context record, but do not merge it with the Xajpani canonical entry until Tai-language interpretation and community-level product identity are better established.

## Household-level field report: Goahai Gaon, Dhemaji

A 2022 GOYA feature reports an in-person xaj-making and tasting visit with **Bulumoni Chetiya Kowar** in **Goahai Gaon, Dhemaji district**. The article describes this as a household account rather than a representative survey. It reports the use of bora (glutinous) rice; a starter cake called **xajor pitha** made from rice flour and locally gathered plants; drying the cakes after about three days under a plant/hay/banana-leaf covering; fermenting rice and powdered starter in an earthen **koloh** for **3–5 days**; then adding water and straining. The author notes that the brewer did not disclose exact herb proportions and that household recipes can vary. These details must stay attributed to this one household, not generalized to all Tai-Ahom brewers. [SRC-060](https://www.goya.in/blog/xaj-rice-beer-of-the-ahoms)

The feature also describes serving etiquette involving a bowl (*kah bati*), banana-leaf covering and sung *nam* (prayer) before guests drink or empty bowls are collected. Treat this as a reported household/family practice, not a universal rule for every Ahom household. The article reports that Kowar stores **rohi**, described as sweet remnants/liquid from fermentation, and offers it for abdominal discomfort. This is a reported household belief, not evidence of clinical efficacy. Keep the term **rohi** source-specific; it should not automatically be equated with the similarly named liquid described in other studies.

The report gives a named village and a household-level observation, which is more geographically specific than a district-only survey. It does not establish village-wide prevalence or current practice beyond the documented visit. The locality is now recorded as `ASM-PL-055`.

### Comparative field study lead: SRC-061

Borah et al. (2021) report ethnobotanical fieldwork across 32 villages in six Assam districts, including Ahom respondents, as part of a four-community study with 202 respondents overall. This is a useful comparative source for traditional beverage and starter-culture knowledge, but the abstract does not identify all villages or establish village-by-village current Xaj production. Use it to guide full-text/table review, not to replace the more specific Goahai Gaon household account or to infer that all Ahom products called Xaj, Haj and Luk-Lau are synonymous. [SRC-061](https://doi.org/10.13057/biodiv/d220451)

### Methodological scope of the 2021 multi-community study

The full-text methods section specifies fieldwork from **October 2019 to March 2020** in 32 villages across **Baksa, Biswanath, Golaghat, Lakhimpur, Sivasagar (printed as Sibasagar) and Sonitpur**. Respondents were selected purposively/stratified and voluntarily; the paper does not provide a village-by-village list in the main text or map each Ahom beverage observation to an individual village. Treat these as the study's overall sampling districts, not proof that Xaj was prepared in every district or every village. [SRC-061](https://doi.org/10.13057/biodiv/d220451)

### Additional naming/locality lead: Haj or Koloh pani (SRC-065)

Bhuyan and Baishya (2013) report an Ahom locality/informant trail associated with **Baragharia village, Golaghat**, and the labels **Haj / Koloh pani**, with **Vekur pitha** as a starter term. This does not prove that Haj/Koloh pani is a synonym for Xajpani, nor does it establish present-day village-wide production. Keep this as a separate naming lead until the underlying source and local terminology are checked. See `ASM-PL-056`. [SRC-065](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dit.2013.09.002)

## Research pass — 2026-10-22: Luk-Lau / Nam-Lau / Lao-pani comparison

### Evidence found

1. **Historical ritual-name evidence:** the British Library Endangered Archives Programme catalogue for the Tai-language manuscript *Khon Ming* (catalogue record EAP373/4/25, dated to the 18th century) says the *Dam Phi* mantra invites deities to receive **Luk Lau**, Phek Khu and Phak Tai at annual observances and life events. This supports the historical presence of the term in a ritual-offering context. The catalogue summary is not a critical translation of the manuscript and does not identify ingredients, preparation, alcohol content or a village-level brewing practice. The digitised manuscript should be read by a qualified Tai/Ahom reader before drawing linguistic conclusions. [SRC-058]

2. **Ethnobotanical/process evidence for a related beverage family:** Saikia, Tag and Das (2007), studying rural Tai-Ahom communities in North Lakhimpur district, describe the rice beverage as **Sajpani (Tai) / Laopani (Assamese)** and discuss its processing and plant materials. The article does not, from the accessible abstract, prove that Luk-Lau or Nam-Lau is an exact synonym of Sajpani/Laopani. [SRC-021; SRC-071]

3. **Modern secondary-name evidence:** Assam Tourism lists Luk-Lau as an Ahom beverage. A 2015 cultural overview lists Luk-Lau, Nam-Lau and Lao-pani among local names, but gives limited preparation-level evidence. A 2025 magazine feature labels “Rohi / Luk-Lao” as concentrated Xaj-pani; this is a useful lead, not sufficient evidence to merge the names because the terms are not consistently aligned across sources. [SRC-047; SRC-051; SRC-072]

4. **Existing Xajpani evidence:** the archive already documents Xajpani/Xaj pani/Koloh pani, Vekur pitha and source-specific process descriptions, including a named household account from Goahai Gaon, Dhemaji. This evidence should remain separate from the historical Luk-Lau name until a Tai/Ahom language source or community knowledge holder establishes the relationship.

### Assessment

- **Luk-Lau exists as a historically recorded name:** supported by the manuscript catalogue summary and modern tourism source.
- **Luk-Lau is a distinct finished beverage with a known recipe:** not established by the sources checked.
- **Luk-Lau = Nam-Lau = Lao-pani = Xajpani/Koloh pani/Haj pani:** not established.
- **Rohi is the same product as Luk-Lau or Xajpani:** not established; source descriptions differ.
- **Village-level current production:** not established specifically for Luk-Lau.

### Research decision

Do not create a new canonical beverage row and do not silently add Luk-Lau to ASM-008-XAJ's confirmed synonym list. Keep it as a historically grounded naming lead attached to the open terminology question. The most valuable next evidence is a qualified reading of the *Khon Ming* manuscript passage, a Tai-Ahom language source defining the terms, or consent-based confirmation from knowledgeable Tai-Ahom speakers/brewers in a named locality.

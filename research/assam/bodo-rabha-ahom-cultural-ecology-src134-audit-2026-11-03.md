# Bodo–Rabha–Ahom cultural ecology audit — SRC-134 (2026-11-03)

## Source identity
Talukdar, S., Jyethi, D. S., Sarma, B., & Talukdar, H. (2026). “Cultural ecology and conservation of traditional beverage practices among selected communities of Assam, Northeast India.” *Discover Food*, 6, article 376. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s44187-026-01101-w. Open-access Version of Record published 4 September 2026.

## Field design and geographic resolution
- Fieldwork stated as February 2023–2024.
- 30 informants: 10 each from Bodo, Rabha and Ahom communities.
- Methods: snowball sampling, semi-structured interviews, participatory rural appraisal, focus-group discussions, participant observation and plant collection.
- Districts: Bodo — Kokrajhar and Baksa; Rabha — Goalpara and Kamrup; Ahom — Tinsukia and Dibrugarh.
- The main text does not name villages, provide district-to-informant allocations, or map particular households/batches to particular districts. No locality records were created from district-level evidence.

## Beverage terminology and process notes
- Bodo: Jou; rice steamed and mixed with herbal starter called angkur; earthen-pot fermentation reported as 5–7 days; a stronger variant may be distilled. Article also refers to starter as Bakhor among Bodo and Rabha.
- Rabha: Choko in abstract/general description; Chubitchi appears in the taboo section. Preserve this inconsistency; do not assume synonymy without community confirmation. Article describes boiled rice and herbal starter, bamboo containers sealed with banana leaves, then filtration.
- Ahom: Xajpani in abstract/general description; also Haaj/Laopani terms in cultural discussion. Starter names include Vekur pitha/Xajpitha; preserve terminology as reported and do not merge with other Ahom beverage records solely on name similarity.
- These are source-level descriptions, not universal recipes for all households or districts.

## Ethnobotanical findings
- Article reports 41 species in 29 families, with 85% wild-sourced and 24 taxa said to be shared by all three communities.
- Plant parts and roles include flavour, aroma, preservation, fermentation assistance and physical barrier/structural functions. The paper reports leaves as the most frequently used part (24 species).
- The ten highest reported CKSI values: *Scoparia dulcis* 0.68; *Zingiber officinale* 0.67; *Tinospora cordifolia* 0.67; *Piper betle* 0.65; *Piper nigrum* 0.64; *Capsicum annuum* 0.59; *Musa paradisiaca* 0.59; *Cinnamomum bejolghota* 0.54; *Equisetum debile* 0.53; *Ananas comosus* 0.50.
- Ten highest reported FL values: *Musa paradisiaca* 3.78; *Zingiber officinale* 3.25; *Mangifera indica* 2.53; *Artocarpus heterophyllus* 2.40; *Psidium guajava* 2.32; *Piper longum* 2.25; *Cinnamomum bejolghota* 2.16; *Vernonia cinerea* 1.90; *Scoparia dulcis* 1.89; *Leucas aspera* 1.70. Values are transcribed as reported, not recalculated.

## Internal consistency and methodological cautions
1. The paper says 41 taxa, but its life-form totals are 17 herbs + 13 shrubs + 9 trees + 4 climbers + 4 ferns = 47. The reported life-form categories therefore sum to six more than the stated species count.
2. The paper defines Fidelity Level as (Ip/Iu) × 100, but reports values such as 3.78 and 3.25. These do not read as percentages under the stated formula. Retain the raw table values and seek author clarification; do not silently rescale.
3. Table 1 includes community-specific and shared labels, but main text does not provide a transparent district-to-sample mapping. Do not infer where each taxon was collected.
4. “Choko” and “Chubitchi”, and “Xajpani”, “Haaj” and “Laopani”, appear in different article sections. The article does not provide a formal synonym reconciliation.
5. Ethnobotanical/ritual reports are not biochemical, clinical or safety validation. No health-benefit claims are added to the beverage records.

# Southern Assam beverage sample audit

**Reviewed:** 2026-10-22  
**Scope:** Haria (Tea-tribe communities), Zao (Zeme Naga), Hmar Zu, Karbi Hor Alank and Dimasa Judima as represented in two multi-community papers.

## Executive finding

Two recent studies strengthen the evidence that these beverage traditions are documented in southern Assam, but neither reviewed text provides a reliable village-by-beverage crosswalk. Keep the existing district-level regional records; do not manufacture village locations from a study-area map or assume each community was sampled in each of the three districts.

## Source A — Yumnam, Hazarika & Sharma (2024)

**Title:** “Metagenomic insights into traditional fermentation of rice-based beverages among ethnic tribes in southern Assam, Northeast India.” *Frontiers in Microbiology* 15:1410098. Published 11 September 2024.  
DOI: https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2024.1410098  
Full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11459095/

- Covers Zeme Naga Zao, Dimasa Kachari Judima, Hmar Zu, Karbi Hor Alank and Tea-tribe Haria.
- Methods describe 15 traditional samples, three per community cohort, collected before distillation.
- The reviewed methods describe the study as southern Assam but do not establish a named village for each beverage sample.
- Table 2 provides broad population/distribution information, not a sample-origin table. Do not treat its district lists as proof that each beverage was collected in every district listed.
- It uses **Zao** in the narrative and **Zou** in a table. Record this as source-level spelling variation, not proof that all named Zeme preparations are identical.
- Microbiome results characterize the collected samples; they do not establish universal recipes, safety, clinical health benefits, or current production in all districts.

## Source B — Yumnam, Nath & Sharma (2026; first published online 29 December 2025)

**Title:** “Brewing Traditions of Northeast India, South Assam: Exploring Rice Beer’s Cultural Significance and Indigenous Craftsmanship.” *Biotechnology and Applied Biochemistry* 73(3):1501–1511.  
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1002/bab.70113  
Full text as author-uploaded: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/399158107_Brewing_Traditions_of_Northeast_India_South_Assam_Exploring_Rice_Beer%27s_Cultural_Significance_and_Indigenous_Craftsmanship

- Methods identify the study period as May–October 2023 and the three study districts as Cachar, Hailakandi and Karimganj. The paper was published after the district was renamed Sribhumi; preserve the historical name in the citation and use a crosswalk for current display.
- The abstract reports 49 households across the overall study and interviews with older knowledge holders. It does not allocate the 49 households by community, district or beverage in the reviewed text.
- Table 1 and beverage subsections describe Judima/Humao, Zao/Nduihi, Haria/Dabai, Hor Alank/Thap and Hmar Zu/Chol. These are useful descriptions of the reported traditions, but not a village-level sample register.
- The Haria subsection reports rice and *Glycyrrhiza glabra* bark for a starter called **Dabai**, and describes a 3–4 day process. Its references include a 2014 study explicitly about Haria in lateritic West Bengal. Until the authors' southern Assam household-level notes or another locality-specific source corroborate it, treat this process as a published description associated with the paper, not a definitive recipe for every Tea-tribe Haria community in Assam.
- The paper reports **Ndhui gei** as not botanically identified. Do not silently assign a species.
- Cultural and medicinal-use statements are ethnographic reports, not clinical evidence.

## Public data and sample-count reconciliation

The 2024 article links a supplementary file named **Data_Sheet_1.docx** and a public NCBI BioProject, **PRJNA1017140**. The BioProject landing page describes bacterial communities from southern Assam rice-based fermented products and currently lists **five SRA experiments and five BioSamples**. The paper's methods, however, report **15 beverage samples, three per community cohort**.

This is a metadata reconciliation question, not proof of an error: the public sequencing records may represent pooled, selected, or otherwise processed samples. The visible BioProject summary does not show a village-to-beverage mapping or explain the five-record versus 15-field-sample relationship. Do not assume one SRA experiment equals one original household/sample, and do not invent geographic attribution from accession counts.

A closer pass through the full text clarifies the distinction between the five reported cohort labels and the 15 physical samples. Section 2.2 says three samples were collected from each community cohort; section 2.5 says each sample was uniquely identified by indexing PCR and the indexed products were combined into a pooled library. Table 3 nevertheless reports one label per cohort—**DMK** (Dimasa Kachari), **HM** (Hmar), **K1** (Karbi), **ZN** (Zeme Naga) and **TT** (Tea tribes)—with pH and electrical-conductivity values expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Section 3.3 also reports read-count ranges by tribe. Thus, the five labels are cohort-level identifiers in the published analysis, not a published list of all 15 original sample IDs. Their matching count with the five public BioSamples/SRA experiments is suggestive but does not establish a one-to-one accession mapping. The article does not provide the accession-to-original-sample crosswalk in the visible text.

## Geographic handling rules

1. Keep Cachar, Hailakandi and Karimganj/Sribhumi as the overall study region, not as confirmed production districts for every listed drink.
2. Do not add village rows unless a source explicitly connects the village to the beverage or the sample.
3. Keep the 2024 study's 15-sample design distinct from the 2023 fieldwork described in the 2026 publication; do not combine their sample counts.
4. Maintain separate records for Hmar Zu, Tiwa Zu/Chu and Zeme Naga Zao/Zou. Similar spellings do not establish shared identity.
5. Keep unresolved starter spellings (e.g. Haria Dabai/Dawai) open until a source tied to the specific community and locality establishes the preferred form.
6. Prioritize supplementary tables, figure captions, sample logs, and community-authorized follow-up for exact household/village attribution.

## Immediate follow-up

- Inspect the 2024 supplementary material for sample IDs or locality metadata not present in the main text.
- Ask whether the authors can share a community-approved district/village crosswalk for each sample without disclosing private household identities.
- For the 2026 paper, seek author clarification on the allocation of 49 households by community and district, and on which process descriptions are based on original interviews versus cited literature.
- Keep the related Assam research gaps open until the evidence is locality-specific and transparent.

**Audit date:** 2026-11-04

**Article:** Porismita Borah (2026), “Documentation of Ethnobotanical Plants Used in the Preparation of Apong, a Traditionally Prepared Rice Beer by the Mishing Tribal Community of Majuli District,” *International Journal of Advanced Research* 14(04):1361–1368. ## 1. Study design and geographic resolution

- The paper reports fieldwork in three randomly selected Mising-inhabited villages in Majuli district, April–October 2024.
- It reports 28 informants, aged 40–70, with emphasis on elderly women and household knowledge. Methods describe semi-structured interviews, informal discussions and transect walks.
- The names of the three villages are not disclosed in the accessible article text. Therefore, this source supports a Majuli district-level evidence record only; it does **not** justify three village/locality rows or village-level allocation of taxa.
- Plant material is said to have been identified using botanical literature/floras and online taxonomic resources; herbarium sheets were submitted to Jengraimukh College. No voucher accession numbers are supplied in the accessible text.
- The paper reports 48 taxa in 30 families. The Results table is numbered 1–48 and all 48 entries are transcribed in `majuli-48-plants-src084.csv`. Two table strings contain extraction spacing (“Centella asiatic a”; “Alstonia scholari s”); these are preserved pending visual verification against the publisher PDF.

## 2. Distinct beverage processes described

The article explicitly treats Nogin Apong and Po:ro Apong as different preparations. Its descriptions should remain source-specific.

### Nogin Apong (article pp. 1363–1364)
- Mostly non-glutinous rice is cleaned, cooked fully, cooled on a bamboo mat and hand-smashed.
- Crushed E’pob starter cake is mixed uniformly with the rice.
- The mixture is placed in an earthen pot lined with banana leaf; the mouth is plugged with paddy straw.
- It is left undisturbed for at least 6–7 days; the article says complete fermentation generally takes 10–15 days in summer.
- Water is poured over fermented rice and the liquid is consumed.
- No ash addition or Ta:suk extraction is described for this Nogin method.

### Po:ro Apong (article p. 1364)
- Dry paddy straw and paddy husk placed over it are partially burnt; the resulting ash is stored.
- Cooked, preferably non-sticky rice is cooled, mixed with the ash and hand-smashed into a dark mass.
- Powdered E’pob is added; the mixture is left on a wooden mat overnight or transferred to an earthen pot, plugged with dried paddy straw, leaves or rukji.
- The article reports 10–15 days for proper fermentation in summer.
- The fermented material is separated using a funnel-shaped bamboo strainer, Ta:suk, with straw at its base. Water is poured over the mash and the filtrate collected as Apong.
- This is filtration/percolation in the account, not distillation. Do not conflate it with other papers that use “distillation” terminology.

### Cross-source process differences that must remain visible
- SRC-136 (Kardong et al. 2012) describes Po:ro Apong/Saimod with Epop, ash, a Ta:suk and about 20 days at 30–35°C.
- SRC-145 (Handique & Deka 2016) explicitly labels its Mising 1:45 starter and 4–5-day summer / 8–9-day winter process as **Nogin Apong**.
- SRC-084 gives 10–15 summer days for both described preparations.
- These differences are not errors to average into one recipe. They may reflect locality, seasonal practice, terminology or reporting differences; source-level evidence does not settle which explanation applies.

## 3. The 48-entry E’pob inventory

The article’s Results table supplies scientific name, vernacular name, family, plant part, RFC and Use Value (UV). The full 48 rows are transcribed in the accompanying CSV. Important controls:
- RFC and UV are the article’s quantitative ethnobotanical indices; they are not measures of chemical concentration, fermentation performance or health effect.
- The paper reports *Oryza sativa* RFC 1.0 / UV 1.6 and *Dryopteris filix-mas* RFC 1.0 / UV 1.5 as the highest UV entries.
- The article describes the list as E’pob/starter-cake plants. Do not assume every entry is used in every household or in both Nogin and Po:ro in the same way.
- Plant parts are transcribed as printed. Some entries and family assignments require botanical review; no voucher IDs are available to resolve them.
- Ethnomedicinal statements in the article are traditional-use reports, not clinical efficacy evidence.

## 4. Preliminary cross-source reconciliation

This audit locks the SRC-084 source transcription first. Crosswalk conclusions below are conservative and are not synonym determinations.

### Directly repeated printed names across prior Mising inventories
- SRC-145 (Handique & Deka 2016, 17-name list): *Artocarpus heterophyllus*, *Saccharum officinarum*, *Oldenlandia corymbosa*, *Ananas comosus*, *Piper longum*, *Centella asiatica* (SRC-084 table spacing issue), *Hydrocotyle sibthorpioides*, *Scoparia dulcis*, *Piper nigrum* and *Swertia* (epithet differs in the printed records and needs checking). These are text/name leads, not evidence of identical plant part or household role.
- SRC-136 (Kardong et al. 2012, Dibrugarh): *Scoparia dulcis*, *Piper longum* and *Piper nigrum* are direct name matches. “Centala acciatica” is a candidate spelling variant of *Centella asiatica*; “Swertia chirata” vs *Swertia chirayita* is unresolved and not silently merged.
- SRC-146 (Gogoi, Dutta & Mondal 2013, Sivasagar): repeated names include *Artocarpus heterophyllus*, *Saccharum officinarum*, *Vitex negundo*, *Oldenlandia corymbosa*, *Ananas comosus*, *Piper longum*, *Centella asiatica*, *Hydrocotyle sibthorpioides*, *Zanthoxylum oxyphyllum*, *Ageratum conyzoides*, *Piper nigrum*, *Cinnamomum tamala*, *Gomphostemma parviflorum*, *Scoparia dulcis*, *Zanthoxylum nitidum*, *Flemingia strobilifera*, *Mikania scandens*, *Azadirachta indica*, *Alpinia nigra*, *Psidium guajava*, *Oryza sativa* and additional candidate matches whose spelling differs (for example “Termalia arjuna” vs *Terminalia arjuna*, “Guilandian bonduc” vs *Guilandina bonduc*, “Alstonia scholari s” vs *Alstonia scholaris*). Exact match status should be based on original page images before taxonomic normalization.
- SRC-005/006/007 remain separate Sivasagar/Lakhimpur/Majuli datasets. The existing SRC-136 and SRC-146 crosswalks record earlier matches, but do not convert their source-specific inventory totals into a statewide unique-species count.

### Explicit non-merges / verification flags
- SRC-084 *Zanthoxylum nitidum* is not SRC-136 “Zanthoxylum hemiltonianum” or SRC-145 *Zanthoxylum hamiltonianum*.
- SRC-084 *Zanthoxylum oxyphyllum* is a separate printed taxon from *Z. nitidum*.
- SRC-084 *Hydrocotyle sibthorpioides* is not *Centella asiatica*; the article lists both separately.
- *Swertia chirayita* and earlier “Swertia chirata” spellings are held as a candidate taxonomic spelling/identity issue pending source-image and botanical-authority review.
- “Tinospora Miers” is genus-level/incomplete as printed; do not assign a species.
- “Aschasma loroglossum” and several other names/family combinations need authoritative taxonomic and voucher review; retain as printed.

## 5. What this source adds to the Assam evidence base

1. It provides a 2024 fieldwork window and a reported 28-informant sample for Majuli, rather than only a literature-derived plant list.
2. It gives a separately reported 48-row inventory and RFC/UV data, including plant parts and vernacular names.
3. It independently documents a no-ash Nogin process and an ash-added Po:ro process, both with Ta:suk specifically in the Po:ro description.
4. It strengthens the evidence that the two Apong preparations should not be merged into a single generic recipe.
5. It does not reveal the three sampled village names, provide voucher accession numbers, or establish a universal recipe or clinical benefit.

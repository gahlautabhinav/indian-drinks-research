# SRC-338 — Bose, Teron & Tamuli (2018): Dimasa Ju / Jumai-Judi / Juhara / Laosa name inventory

**Audit date:** 22 November 2026  
**Source:** Sanghamitra Das Bose, Robindra Teron and Ajit Kumar Tamuli (2018), “Wild Plant Resources Used as Food by the Dimasas of Karbi-Anglong District, Assam, India,” *International Journal of Pure and Applied Bioscience* 6(4):158–163. DOI: 10.18782/2320-7051.6704.  
**Full text:** https://www.ijpab.com/form/2018%20Volume%206%2C%20issue%204/IJPAB-2018-6-4-158-163.pdf

## 1. Why this source matters

The article explicitly reports that Dimasa alcoholic drinks are called “Ju, Jumai/Judi, Juhara and Laosa” and says the names vary with fermentation procedure, ingredients and taste. It singles out Jumai as a variety that can be extracted in excess from “Baireng” rice and says it is more commonly used than the other varieties.

This is the clearest retrieved source so far that explicitly lists all four forms together as Dimasa beverage names. It is still not a beverage-specific ethnographic study, and it does not provide a recipe or process for each name.

## 2. Study design and locality boundary

The study's stated research question is wild plant resources used as food by Dimasas in Karbi-Anglong. Fieldwork ran March 2015–February 2016 and covered 12 villages and associated forest lands. The methods name Dogju Pathar, Nilapur, Hojajpur, Khakhao, Longflaidisha, Lailingdisha, Tamulbari, Khijurbon and one village of Manja; the abstract says 12 villages, while the methods' named list is not a complete 12-place list. Interviews were with elderly people recommended by the Khunang (village head) and female vendors. Herbarium vouchers of plant parts were secured.

The 12-village frame is for wild-food plant research. The paragraph naming Ju/Jumai/Juhara/Laosa does not allocate those beverages or their producers to any of those villages. Do not add the 12 study villages as Judima/Jumai/Juhara/Laosa production localities.

## 3. What is documented — and what is not

### Supported at source level

- Four Dimasa drink names are printed together: Ju, Jumai/Judi, Juhara and Laosa.
- The article says the differences relate to fermentation procedure, ingredients and taste.
- Jumai is said to be extractable in larger quantity from Baireng rice and to be more commonly used than the other forms.
- Humao is described as essential in all kinds of indigenous rice-beer preparation.
- The article says small pieces of *Acacia pennata* bark (“Themra”) are used in starter cakes.
- Table 1 row 42 independently lists *Acacia pennata*, local name “Sam Themra,” bark, mode “Fermentation,” and says dried bark is added to prepare the starter cake necessary for Judima.

### Not established

- No separate ingredient list, fermentation time, extraction apparatus, alcohol strength or sensory profile is supplied for each of the four drink names.
- It does not state whether “Ju” is an umbrella term or one particular preparation; it does not define whether Jumai/Judi are synonyms or separate forms.
- It does not explicitly equate Juhara with the Juharo/Juharao spellings in other sources, although the resemblance makes this a plausible crosswalk lead.
- It does not provide a separate Laosa preparation or establish its relationship to Juhara.
- It does not establish that Jumai is the same as Judima or Bodo Jumai/Jou.
- The “Baireng” rice claim is attached to Jumai in this article; do not silently transfer it to every Judima preparation or assume the NIPCCD “maiju hanger mairung” is the same cultivar.

## 4. Cross-source name and process controls

### 4.1 Jumai is a collision-prone name

Arjun (2015), SRC-143, studies Judima, Jumai, Horo and Poro in a mixed field frame spanning Karbi, Mising and Dimasa people and North Cachar Hills/Karbi Anglong. Its abstract maps Poro, Horo, Judima and Jumai in an ambiguous sequence to Mising, Karbi, Dimasa and Boro respectively. The methods then describe the four beverages in a combined way without a reliable beverage-to-community sample allocation. Do not use SRC-143 as proof that Dimasa Jumai is Judima or that Bodo Jumai is the Dimasa product.

Loying et al. (2024), SRC-020, uses “Jumai” in the section on Bodo Jou Bishi: it describes the cooked-rice/starter blend as Jumai before it is placed in a pot to yield Jou Bishi. In that context Jumai is a process-stage term in a Bodo recipe, not evidence about the Dimasa beverage called Jumai in Bose et al. (2018).

### 4.2 Dictionary cross-check is not enough

The XOBDO entry for Assamese “asab” lists a Dimasa translation cluster “ju gjao,” “zu,” and “zudima, zuharo, laosa, zugab.” This is a broad dictionary entry for the generic concept “alcohol/arrack,” not a beverage taxonomy. It does not list Jumai/Judi and cannot establish that Juharo, Laosa, Zugab and Judima are synonyms. Treat as a lexical discovery lead only; the dictionary page itself says it is a community-contributed dictionary and cites the Assamese Chandrakanta dictionary as reference for the headword.

### 4.3 Juhara/Juharo and distilled form

SRC-304, SRC-336 and SRC-337 describe Juharo/Juharao as distilled liquor but differ or omit details about the still charge. Bose et al. (2018) prints “Juhara” without a process. Preserve the spelling “Juhara” as printed until a Dimasa-language source or producer confirms its relation to Juharo/Juharao.

### 4.4 Laosa remains unresolved

No independent beverage-specific process description for Laosa was found in this retrieval pass. Do not create a separate canonical beverage record, recipe or production locality based solely on its inclusion in this name list.

## 5. Archive decision

1. Register the article as SRC-338.
2. Preserve the source's four-name inventory exactly: Ju; Jumai/Judi; Juhara; Laosa.
3. Record Baireng as a source-specific rice association for Jumai only.
4. Record *Acacia pennata* / “Sam Themra” as a plant-starter association reported by the paper, without assigning any of its 12 wild-food villages to beverage production.
5. Keep Jumai distinct from Bodo Jumai/Jou and from Judima until primary producer/linguistic evidence resolves the names.
6. Keep Juhara as a spelling lead, not an automatic synonym of Juharo/Juharao.
7. Keep Laosa as an unresolved beverage name with no recipe or locality assignment.
8. Continue seeking community-level Dimasa-language sources and producer accounts that explicitly distinguish each name and process.

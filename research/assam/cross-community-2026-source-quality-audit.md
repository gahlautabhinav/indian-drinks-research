# Assam cross-community study SRC-091: source-quality and claim-scope audit

**Reviewed:** 2026-10-23  
**Source:** Talukdar et al. (2026), *Cultural ecology and conservation of traditional beverage practices among selected communities of Assam, Northeast India*, *Discover Food* 6, article 376. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s44187-026-01101-w  
**Archive source ID:** SRC-091  
**Purpose:** Record what the study can support, where it should not be over-interpreted, and the exact follow-up needed before it changes canonical beverage or locality records.

## 1. Scope and methods reported by the paper

The paper reports fieldwork during February 2023–2024 in six districts: Kokrajhar and Baksa for Bodo participants, Goalpara and Kamrup for Rabha participants, and Tinsukia and Dibrugarh for Ahom participants. It reports 30 informants in total (10 per community), recruited using snowball sampling, and methods including semi-structured interviews, focus-group discussions, participant observation, and plant collection/identification. It reports 41 plant species across 29 families, with 85% wild-sourced.

These are **study-sample and study-area facts**, not a census of all beverage makers or all beverage-producing villages. The six districts cannot be added to a beverage's confirmed production geography without the paper explicitly linking a beverage and practice to a named site.

## 2. Claim audit

| Claim in source | What can be retained | Limitation / required handling |
|---|---|---|
| 41 plant species across 29 families | The paper reports this total for the combined study | Do not treat it as a harmonized cross-community taxonomic dataset until Table 1, plant names, duplicate taxa and identifications are checked |
| 85% of species wild-sourced | A reported aggregate finding for this study | Do not generalize to every beverage, community, locality, season or current practice |
| Six districts across Bodo, Rabha and Ahom fieldwork | District-level study coverage as reported in Methods | Does not prove every named beverage is made in every district, and does not identify village-level production |
| Jou, Choko and Xajpani use different plant parts and starter cultures | A useful comparative lead | The article's introductory/general preparation wording must not override beverage-specific and community-specific sources. Do not merge starter terms (e.g., Amao/Emao, Bakhor/Phap, Vekur pitha) across communities without direct evidence |
| Plant use, ritual restrictions and conservation benefits | Source-attributed ethnographic reports from participants and authors | Keep as reported practices/interpretations, not universal rules or independently demonstrated causal conservation effects. Do not generalize sensitive or restrictive customs to every household |
| Medicinal/therapeutic value | The paper reports community use and discusses potential | Ethnobotanical use is not proof of clinical efficacy, dosage, safety, or a health benefit from the beverage itself |

## 3. Internal numerical and interpretation checks

### 3.1 Life-form counts

The results text reports 17 herbs, 13 shrubs, 9 trees, 4 climbers and 4 ferns. These category counts sum to **47**, whereas the paper reports **41 species**. The categories might overlap (for example, a fern can also be categorized by growth form), or the count may reflect a reporting/transcription issue. The paper text reviewed does not explain the discrepancy. Do not silently normalize or reproduce the life-form breakdown as mutually exclusive categories until Table 1 and the authors' classification are checked.

### 3.2 Fidelity Level (FL) values

The paper defines FL as a percentage multiplied by 100, but reports values such as 3.78 for *Musa paradisiaca*, 3.25 for *Zingiber officinale* and 2.53 for *Mangifera indica*, and describes these as high/strong consensus. These values fall within a 0–100 percentage range, but if they are percentages, the numerical values are low rather than high. The paper's interpretation therefore appears inconsistent with its stated formula or scale. Do not describe these values as strong consensus or compare them with standard FL percentages without checking the original table, formula, denominator and any author correction.

### 3.3 Plant name and table reconciliation

The article's results and discussion associate some plant names with community-specific use, but this is not enough by itself to update the archive's botanical reconciliation tables. Before making taxon-level changes, compare the full Table 1 and supplementary material against the archive's existing source-specific inventories, preserving:
- exact spelling as printed;
- plant part and stated function;
- community/site attribution as printed;
- identification authority or voucher information if available;
- unresolved spelling, synonym and family conflicts.

Do not merge taxa solely because vernacular or scientific spellings resemble one another.

## 4. Safe repository use

This source is appropriate as:
- a recent comparative source lead for Bodo, Rabha and Ahom traditions;
- evidence that the authors studied selected communities in the six named districts;
- a source-attributed report of plant-use and cultural-practice themes.

It is **not sufficient alone** for:
- adding a confirmed production village to any beverage;
- declaring that every district in the study area produces every beverage named in the paper;
- merging beverage or starter-culture records;
- replacing community-specific recipes with a generic recipe;
- validating health or therapeutic claims;
- treating the paper's reported FL values as high consensus without clarification.

## 5. Follow-up actions

1. Extract Table 1 and supplementary plant data into a source-specific comparison, not directly into the canonical inventory.
2. Check the 41-species total against the reported life-form categories and table rows.
3. Recalculate or query the FL values only if the original informant-level counts and denominator are available; otherwise preserve the paper's reported numbers and flag the interpretation.
4. Review community-specific result rows for exact links between plant use, beverage, and study district; do not infer village distribution.
5. If contacting authors, ask for clarification of the life-form count and FL scale, and whether a corrected table/version is available.

## 5. Beverage-to-plant attribution audit — 2026-10-23

The source-faithful transcription of Table 1 is archived at data/src091_table1_transcription.csv. Its columns include local name, community, voucher, scientific name, life form, family, plant part, CKSI and FL, but **no field identifying the specific finished beverage or starter-culture product to which each plant row belongs**. The article's general-method prose mentions Jou, Choko and Xajpani, while Table 1 attributes plants to Ahom, Bodo and/or Rabha communities. These two kinds of information must not be joined into plant-by-beverage claims without additional evidence.

Practical interpretation:
- A plant tagged “Ahom” is community-attributed in the paper; this alone does not prove it is used in every Ahom beverage or every Xajpani preparation.
- A plant tagged “Bodo; Rabha” cannot be assigned to Jou and Choko separately unless a source passage explicitly makes that connection.
- Shared plant rows may support a broad cross-community ethnobotanical comparison, but not a complete ingredient list for any single beverage.
- The article's stated total of 24 species common to all three communities should be checked against the table's community labels before reproducing as a calculated archive result.

This is a separate evidence limitation from the life-form-count discrepancy, FL-scale issue, and duplicate voucher identifier. It keeps SRC-091 useful as a comparative ethnobotanical source without allowing over-specific recipe claims.

### Required follow-up
Request the authors' underlying plant-by-community-by-beverage table or field-note mapping, if it exists and can be shared ethically. Until then, use the Table 1 transcription as community-level plant evidence only, and preserve the missing beverage-level link as an open research gap.

# SRC-219 — Gogoi et al. (2026): Mising fermented foods and beverages in Jorhat district

**Citation:** Gogoi P, Konwar S, Das S, Das D, Doimari R, Sonar T, Doley S. 2026. “Ethnobotanical documentation of medicinal plants and traditional fermented foods of the Mishing community in Jorhat district of Assam, India.” *Ethnobotany Research and Applications* 34:75, 1–31. DOI: https://doi.org/10.32859/era.34.75.1-31

**Primary full text:** https://ethnobotanyjournal.org/index.php/era/article/download/7814/2403/80765  
**Article landing page:** https://ethnobotanyjournal.org/index.php/era/article/view/7814

## Why this source matters

This is a new primary field study directly covering Mising communities in Jorhat district. The full paper reports field visits to ten named villages between March 2024 and 2025, interviews and group discussions with 50 informants, and documentation of two rice-based beverages: Po:ro Apong and Nogin Apong. It therefore adds contemporary, named-village fieldwork to the Jorhat evidence base, beyond the older SRC-148 and the still-unretrieved SRC-149.

The paper's scope is mixed ethnomedicinal and fermented-food documentation. Its headline count of 94 medicinal plant species across 56 families is **not** a count of Apong starter taxa. The conclusion separately states that 20 plant species were used in starter cultures for Po:ro and Nogin Apong.

The authors state that they visited the villages during the first week of each month for 12 consecutive months, with fieldwork described as March 2024–2025. They report 50 informants and describe purposive and snowball selection, including traditional healers, elderly people and general community members. The abstract says 50 key informants; the results describe 17 men and 33 women.

**Geographic boundary:** the article identifies the ten villages as study sites in Jorhat district, but does not provide a village-by-village beverage observation matrix, coordinates or revenue-village codes in the accessible text. Do not create ten beverage-specific place records or assign either beverage to each village merely because the village was included in the wider ethnobotanical survey.

## Beverage evidence and process descriptions

### Po:ro Apong

The paper (printed pp. 23–24) describes Po:ro as a Mising rice beer using a starter cake called “opo” or “sajpani” in this account. It lists the following plant names in its Po:ro description: *Zingiber officinale*, *Ananas comosus*, *Centella asiatica*, *Pteridium aquilinum*, *Oryza sativa*, *Leucas aspera*, *Piper nigrum*, *Costus speciosus*, *Artocarpus heterophyllus*, *Lygodium flexuosum*, *Saccharum officinarum*, *Drymaria cordata*, *Psidium guajava*, *Murraya koenigii*, *Vitex negundo*, *Piper longum*, *Hydrocotyle rotundifolia*, *Cinnamomum tamala*, *Phlogacanthus thyrsiformis* and *Solanum torvum*. The paragraph ends with “etc.”; although the conclusion reports 20 starter-culture plant species across Po:ro and Nogin, the article does not supply a closed, species-by-beverage matrix. Do not treat the paragraph as a fully validated inventory or assign every listed taxon to both beverage types.

The described process is:
- Partially burn paddy husk and dried paddy straw to collect ash.
- Mix boiled rice with ash and powdered starter cake; place in a fumigated earthen pot and cover with straw and *Thelypteris parasitica* leaves.
- Ferment for roughly one to two weeks, with duration said to depend on starter amount and season/temperature.
- Transfer the fermented mash to a conical bamboo basket (“Ta: suk”) lined with banana leaves, with paddy straw (“Amrong”) at the base as a filter.
- Add water from above and collect the filtrate. The account describes cold water followed by gradually added hot water.

### Nogin Apong

The paper (printed p. 25) describes cooked rice mixed with powdered Apop starter cake, fermented in an earthen pot for 7–10 days. The fermented rice is called “Noginarug”; water is mixed and stirred into it, and the milky liquid is sieved to obtain Nogin Apong.

### Apop / Apoppitha

The starter-cake account (printed pp. 25–26) says rice grain is mixed with dried medicinal leaves, pounded into a sticky paste, shaped into round or oval cakes, and dried for 3–5 days on straw/leaves or a bamboo platform above the fireplace. It describes covering the cakes with “Rukji” (*Amphineuron opulentum*) and paddy straw, then storing them in earthen pots or bamboo containers near the fireplace.

The paper uses several starter names (“opo”, “sajpani”, “Apop”, “Apoppitha”). Preserve the spellings and contexts as source-reported; do not automatically equate every term with all other sources' Epop/Epoppitha terminology.

## Important limitations and internal checks

1. **No voucher specimens:** the authors explicitly state that no voucher specimens were collected or deposited in a recognized herbarium, and no herbarium accession numbers are available (printed p. 5). Plant names are therefore source-reported identifications without voucher-backed verification in this study.
2. **No laboratory beverage analysis:** the authors state that microbiological, phytochemical and pharmacological analyses were beyond the study's scope and that microbial diversity in the fermented foods/beverages was not investigated under laboratory conditions (printed p. 28). Do not extract microbial species, alcohol concentrations, biochemical measurements or clinical efficacy from this paper.
3. **No beverage-by-village allocation:** ten villages are named, but the paper does not say which village(s) supplied Po:ro or Nogin observations, which informants prepared each beverage, or whether all ten villages prepared both.
4. **Starter plant attribution:** the conclusion says 20 species were used for starter cultures of Po:ro and Nogin together. The article does not present a clear split by beverage. The Po:ro paragraph lists names but ends with “etc.” and should not be treated as a definitive, exhaustive 20-row dataset.
5. **Consent wording:** the methods section says prior consent/ethical guidance are “under consideration” (printed p. 4), while a later methods passage and the declarations state that prior informed consent (PIC) was obtained. Preserve the paper's later PIC declaration, but flag this internal wording inconsistency rather than independently certifying the consent process.
6. **Publication date discrepancy:** the article landing page gives 22 June 2026; the PDF header gives 21 June 2026. The citation year and DOI are unaffected.
7. **Spelling/terminology:** the title and body vary between “Mishing” and “Mising”; the text uses “Po: ro”, “Po:ro”, “Noggin” and “Nogin”. Preserve source strings in exact-name crosswalks before normalization.

## Archive decisions

- Register as SRC-219, a primary 2026 Jorhat field study.
- Keep its 94 medicinal-plant total separate from the 20 starter-culture plant claim.
- Do not add beverage-specific place rows for the ten villages until beverage observation is allocated to a named site.
- Do not create a closed 20-taxon CSV from the prose list; obtain author clarification or supplementary material for the exact list and beverage allocation.
- Do not treat the study as a microbiological or biochemical source.
- Keep SRC-148's missing Table 1 and SRC-149's missing Neog paper/MRP report as separate unresolved primary-document gaps.

# SRC-314 — Borah et al. (2021): four-community Assam beverage plant compendium

**Audit date:** 20 November 2026  
**Citation:** Dipankar Borah, Tridip Gogoi, Jintu Sarma, Punam Jyoti Borah, Bichitra Gohain, Chiran jib Mili, Ankur Upadhyaya, Jenima Basumatary, Kasturi Neog, Tonlong Wangpan and Sumpam Tangjang (2021), “Compendium of plants used for preparation of traditional alcoholic beverages by four major ethnic communities of Assam, northeast India,” *Biodiversitas* 22(4):2019–2031. DOI: 10.13057/biodiv/d220451.  
**Full PDF:** https://www.smujo.id/biodiv/article/download/8097/4751/40267  
**Publisher article page:** https://smujo.id/biodiv/article/view/8097

## 1. Fieldwork and scope

The authors conducted field investigations from October 2019 to March 2020 in **32 villages across six Assam districts**:

- Baksa
- Biswanath
- Golaghat
- Lakhimpur
- Sivasagar (spelled “Sibasagar” in parts of the article)
- Sonitpur

They interviewed **202 voluntary respondents**, mainly older knowledge holders and women, using purposive stratified sampling, semi-structured questionnaires and personal interviews/participatory rural appraisal. Plant material was collected in the presence of respondents and processed using the cited Jain and Rao method. Identification used botanical literature and comparisons with voucher specimens at regional herbaria ARUN and ASSAM. The authors state that specimens were submitted to the Herbarium of Rajiv Gandhi University, Arunachal Pradesh.

The paper does **not** name all 32 villages in the main article text, provide a village-by-village respondent count, or publish a complete respondent/sample-to-community crosswalk. The 202 respondents are the total across the four communities, not 202 per community.

Communities covered:
- Ahom
- Bodo
- Karbi
- Mishing

This is a 2019–2020 field survey, not a current prevalence census.

## 2. Plant inventory

The study records **129 plant species, 107 genera and 54 families** associated with traditional alcoholic beverage preparation among the four communities.

The authors state that:
- 39 species had been reported by previous workers.
- 90 were newly recorded in this four-community use dataset.
- “Newly recorded” means new in the study's documented community-use context, not newly discovered botanical species.
- Lamiaceae and Lauraceae were the largest families, with eight species each; Asteraceae had seven; Rubiaceae and Polygonaceae had six each.
- Leaves were the dominant plant part (88%), followed by twigs (4%) and roots (3%).

The paper's supplementary Table S1 is important: it records botanical names, family, vernacular names, plant part, voucher codes, community use codes and earlier literature references. The supplement should be preserved as a distinct evidence asset and checked against the herbarium accession register before individual plant records are promoted into the archive. The main PDF's text does not provide the full voucher catalogue or the complete village-to-plant matrix.

Examples of species described in the paper's discussion as among the commonly added plants include *Ananas comosus*, *Capsicum annuum*, *Centella asiatica*, *Clerodendrum infortunatum*, *Lygodium flexuosum*, *Oryza sativa*, *Piper nigrum*, *Saccharum officinarum* and *Scoparia dulcis*. These are study-level examples, not a universal recipe and not evidence that each community or each village uses every species.

## 3. Source-specific beverage and starter terminology

Table 2 presents the following community-level terminology:

| Community | Beverage names printed | Starter names printed |
|---|---|---|
| Ahom | Haaj, Laupani | Haajpitha, Vekurpitha |
| Bodo | Jou, Bishi | Amao, Angkur |
| Karbi | Hor / Hor-alank | Thap |
| Mishing | Poro apong, Sai mod | Apoppitha |

Figure 3's caption identifies the pictured Ahom beverage as “Xaajpani of Ahom”, whereas Table 2 prints “Haaj, Laupani”. The paper does not explicitly resolve whether Haaj, Laupani and Xaajpani are exact synonyms. Preserve the source's labels and do not merge them automatically.

Likewise, Table 2 prints Bodo “Jou, Bishi”, not the compound “Jou bishi”. The archive retains Bishi as a source-specific label rather than treating it as automatically identical to Jou bishi or any GI-specific Jou product.

For Mishing, the article explicitly links Poro apong and Sai mod in Table 2 and describes a special ash-based preparation/extraction process for Po:ro apong. This is useful corroboration, but does not remove the need to retain local variants and source-specific methods.

## 4. General starter-cake preparation

The article's generalized process describes:
1. Collect plant parts used by the community; wash and use fresh or dried.
2. Dry some plant material in sun or smoke for a few days.
3. Soak rice for approximately 2–6 hours, rinse and grind into powder.
4. Powder dried plant parts separately and mix with the rice powder.
5. Add a small amount of finely powdered old starter cake as inoculum.
6. Form small round balls/cakes.
7. Dry and store the starter cakes above a fireplace.

The article says dried leaves of *Thelypteris parasitica*, *Musa balbisiana* and paddy hay were used to keep starter balls free from pests/moisture and improve storage longevity. It does not assign each covering material to every community or all 32 villages.

## 5. Generic rice-beer preparation

The generalized method in the paper is:
- Boil rice, preferably a glutinous variety.
- Spread cooked rice on banana leaves in a winnowing basket and cool to room temperature.
- Mix powdered starter cake into the rice.
- Transfer to an earthen pot or other vessel.
- Seal with banana or *Alpinia* leaves, straw and a clean cloth.
- Ferment for approximately 5–9 days, varying with season and local climate.
- Remove and filter the fermented mass to separate liquid from rice solids.

This is a generalized account, not a recipe for every named beverage. It should not overwrite the more detailed community-specific methods in primary sources.

## 6. Po:ro apong — distinct ash-based method

The paper gives a separate Mishing Po:ro apong method:

1. Partially burn paddy husk and dried paddy straw and collect the ash separately.
2. Mix the ash and powdered starter cake with boiled rice.
3. Transfer the mixture into a heavily fumigated/blackened earthen pot.
4. Seal the mouth with straw and leaves of *Thelypteris parasitica*.
5. Ferment for about one to two weeks, depending on season, climate and starter quantity.
6. Extract through a cone-shaped bamboo basket called **Ta:suk**. The inside is lined with banana leaves and a pack of paddy straw called **Amrong** is placed at the base as a filter.
7. Pour cold water first, then warm/hot water sequentially through the fermented mixture; collect the filtrate (called Apong in the process description) below the basket.

The paper describes this as filtration/extraction. It is **not distillation**. The ash-based method is the key distinguishing process for Po:ro apong in this source. Do not apply it to Nogin Apong or all Mishing Apong.

The authors state that Po:ro apong is comparatively laborious and is generally prepared for large or important community occasions. This is an author-reported cultural observation, not a current frequency survey.

## 7. Distillation

The article reports observing distillation among the Bodo, Karbi and Mishing communities, but not the Ahom in this survey.

It describes a three-part apparatus:
- Lower chamber: metal vessel holding diluted fermented rice beer.
- Middle chamber: perforated-base earthen pot, with a small receiver bowl or sometimes a bamboo outlet tube.
- Upper chamber: metal vessel holding cold water as condenser.

The joints are sealed with moist muslin, mud or rice-beer residue. Heating generates vapour, which condenses and is collected. The article says the complete process may take about four hours.

The source does not provide a product-specific distilled name for each of the three communities in this generalized apparatus section. Do not infer that every named Bodo, Karbi or Mishing beverage is distilled, or use this generic description to collapse Hor-alank and Hor-arak or Jou and Jou Gwran.

## 8. Cross-source interpretation and corrections

### Ahom
- SRC-314 Table 2 reports Haaj/Laupani and Haajpitha/Vekurpitha.
- Figure 3 caption labels the photographed Ahom beverage Xaajpani.
- Existing ASM-008-XAJ remains Xajpani; SRC-314 is attached as a terminology cross-check, not proof that every label is interchangeable.

### Bodo
- SRC-314 reports Jou/Bishi with Amao/Angkur.
- Keep Bishi distinct from the compound Jou bishi until local usage is verified.
- Do not infer the GI subtype of the beverage from this table.

### Karbi
- SRC-314 reports Hor/Hor-alank with Thap and general distillation among Karbi.
- Existing Hor-alank and Hor-arak records remain separate; the source does not name the distilled product in its general apparatus section.

### Mishing
- SRC-314 explicitly associates Poro apong/Sai mod with Apoppitha and provides the ash-based Ta:suk method.
- This supports the existing ASM-003-POR record. It does not mean every Apong is Poro Apong or uses ash.

## 9. Limitations and safety

- The 32 villages are not individually named in the main article text.
- Respondent numbers by community and village are not supplied.
- There is no complete village-by-plant or village-by-beverage sample matrix in the main article.
- The 129-plant inventory is aggregated across four communities.
- The supplementary Table S1 voucher codes should be checked against the herbarium's actual accession catalogue before creating individual verified plant records.
- No finished-beverage alcohol concentrations, microbial measurements, toxin assays or comprehensive safety tests are reported.
- Ethnomedicinal and flavour/nutrient explanations are traditional knowledge or author interpretations, not clinical efficacy or toxicological proof.
- Do not convert the 129-species inventory into a universal starter recipe or recommendation to ingest any plant.

**Conclusion:** SRC-314 is a major Assam ethnobotanical field source: 202 respondents, 32 villages, six districts, four communities and 129 plant species. Its value is strongest at the aggregated community/source level. It cannot yet support 32 village-level beverage records because the village list and sample-to-community/locality crosswalk are not published in the article text. The supplementary voucher table is the next high-value asset to recover.

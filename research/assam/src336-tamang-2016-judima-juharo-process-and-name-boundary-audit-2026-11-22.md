# SRC-336 — Springer 2016 Judima/Juharo process and Dimasa name-boundary audit

**Audit date:** 22 November 2026  
**Source:** Tamang, J. P., Thapa, N., Bhalla, T. C., & Savitri (2016), “Ethnic Fermented Foods and Beverages of India,” Chapter 2 in *Ethnic Fermented Foods and Alcoholic Beverages of Asia*, edited by J. P. Tamang, Springer India, pp. 17–72. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/978-81-322-2800-4_2  
**Accessible full text:** https://ndl.ethernet.edu.et/bitstream/123456789/74085/1/2016_Book_EthnicFermentedFoodsAndAlcohol.pdf

## 1. Why this source matters

The chapter provides a more explicit process boundary between the fermented liquid called Judima and the distilled product called Juharo than the short Juharo passages in some other sources. It says that after Judima juice is collected, the remaining fermented rice mass (printed as “jugap”) is distilled to make Juharo. This is a material process detail, but it remains a secondary synthesis citing Chakrabarty et al. (2014), not a new field survey.

## 2. Source-faithful process description

In section 2.15.4 (printed page 61–62), the chapter states:

- Judima is a mild alcoholic beverage with a sweet aroma, prepared from steamed glutinous rice in Assam.
- Dimasa sticky-rice cultivar names are printed as “Bairong,” “Maiju-walao,” “Maiju-walao-gedeba” and “Maiju-hadi.”
- Cooked rice is cooled on bamboo mats or banana leaves; approximately 1% Humao is mixed in with a little water.
- The fermenting mass is wrapped in banana leaves or polythene and fermented for 1–2 days in summer or 3–4 days in winter.
- The saccharified mass is transferred to a bamboo cone called khulu (or a hollow earthen pot); a vessel called hundy is placed below to collect the liquid over 1–2 days. That collected liquid is called Judima.
- After collection of Judima, the remaining rice mass, called “jugap,” is distilled to make high-alcohol liquor called Juharo.
- Juharo is described as more alcoholic and acidic in taste than Judima.

The text therefore describes Juharo as a downstream distillate of the residual fermented rice mass, not simply as the same collected liquid called Judima after distillation. Preserve this as the 2016 chapter's process account; do not assume all households use identical equipment, times or sequence.

## 3. Comparison with other sources and unresolved divergences

### 3.1 Juharo process

- **SRC-304 (Chakrabarty, Sharma & Tamang 2011):** describes Biron rice mixed with Humao, fermented 5–6 days, filtered through cloth, then the filtrate is boiled and distilled. This differs materially from SRC-336's account that the residual rice mass (“jugap”) is distilled after liquid Judima is collected.
- **SRC-138 (Arjun et al. 2014):** says Juharao is produced by distillation of Judimah using a crude still, but the reviewed passage does not specify whether the collected liquid, residual solids, or both are charged to the still.
- **SRC-335 (Parbo & Kumar 2021):** identifies Juharo as distilled liquor but does not separately describe its distillation method.
- **SRC-336 (Tamang et al. 2016):** explicitly identifies “jugap” (leftover rice after Judima extraction) as the distillation feedstock.

These are distinct published process descriptions. They should not be collapsed into one standard recipe without a source-level clarification or contemporary producer evidence.

### 3.2 Ju-dima / Ju-haro naming

Hasnu and Bhattacharjee (2016), *South Asian Anthropologist* 16(1):119–121, p. 121, describe “ju-dima” and “ju-haro” as two kinds of Ju; the former is sweet and the latter sourer and stronger. That short material-culture communication does not explicitly describe distillation in the passage. It is not enough to overturn the more process-explicit accounts that classify Juharo/Juharao as distilled liquor.

### 3.3 Other Dimasa beverage names

Bose, Teron and Tamuli (2018), in a wild-food ethnobotany paper, state that Dimasa alcoholic drinks vary with fermentation procedure, ingredients and taste and list “Ju,” “Jumai/Judi,” “Juhara” and “Laosa.” However, this paper's actual study is a 12-village wild-food plant survey in Karbi Anglong, not a beverage survey; it gives no definitions or processes for those four drink names in the accessible text. Do not register these as four validated beverage products or assign them to the 12 food-survey villages. Treat them as an unverified terminology lead requiring a beverage-specific source.

## 4. Evidence limits

- SRC-336 is a synthesis chapter, not new fieldwork; the process is source-attributed through the chapter's citations and should be checked against the underlying primary account.
- It provides no village, household, producer, batch, still design or sample allocation for Judima/Juharo.
- The chapter's broad opening sentence says “Judima or zu” and lists names from different communities in Assam; this is a comparative heading, not proof that Judima and every community's “Zu” are identical products.
- The chapter's process durations (1–2 / 3–4 days plus 1–2 days of collection) differ from SRC-304's Judima and Juharo durations. Keep them source-specific.
- The chapter's mention of perceived postnatal/medicinal use is cultural reporting, not clinical evidence or safety advice.
- No new locality, village, producer, recipe standard, starter ingredient or taxon record is created from this source.

## 5. Archive decision

1. Register the chapter as SRC-336.
2. Add its “jugap” residual-mass distillation account to the Dimasa beverage process comparison, with an explicit conflict note against SRC-304's filtrate-distillation account.
3. Retain Juharo/Juharao spelling variants as source-published forms; do not normalize the spelling without a community/language authority.
4. Keep “Jumai/Judi” and “Laosa” as unresolved name leads only; do not create canonical beverage entities or locality assignments.
5. Continue the next investigation with the underlying primary sources and a dedicated producer/terminology record for Juharo/Judi/Laosa.

## 6. Later cross-check: NIPCCD Dimasa red/white Judima entries and the “Zukap” term — 22 November 2026

The NIPCCD *An Exploratory Study on Indigenous Foods among the Tribals of North Eastern Region* (canonical archive source SRC-049; 2022 institutional index date) documents two Dimasa preparations on printed pages 102–103: “Maiju hanger judima (Red rice beer)” and “Judima (White rice beer).” Both use a starter printed “Xumai”; the fermented rice mass is called “Zukap,” and the liquor extracted from it is Judima. The report does not assign either entry to a named village, respondent or batch. See `research/assam/src049-nipccd-dimasa-judima-xumai-zukap-audit-2026-11-22.md`.

This creates a significant lexical/process comparison with the chapter's printed “jugap”:
- NIPCCD “Zukap”: fermented rice mass from which Judima is extracted.
- SRC-336 “jugap”: residual rice mass after liquid Judima has been collected, reported as the Juharo distillation feedstock.

The spellings may be related, but the source-defined process roles are not identical. Do not normalize “Zukap” and “jugap” as the same technical term or treat NIPCCD as confirming the chapter's distillation feedstock. Likewise, do not equate NIPCCD “Xumai” with Humao/Umhu without a Dimasa-language authority or producer confirmation.

## 7. Further source comparison: Juharo distillation and botanical divergence

Terangpi et al. (2013), now SRC-337, state that *Acacia pennata* (“Themra”) bark is used in Dimasa Humao starter cakes and that Juharao is sometimes produced by distillation of Judimah using a crude still. The paper is principally a bark pharmacognosy study; it does not identify the still charge or a beverage-producing village. It supports its own Acacia identification procedure using a comparator voucher at Assam University, Diphu, but does not publish an accession number or prove the market bark used for analysis came from the voucher specimen. Full audit: `research/assam/src337-terangpi-2013-acacia-pennata-dimasa-humao-judimah-audit-2026-11-22.md`.

The Slow Food Foundation's Judima Ark of Taste page separately describes Humao as rice flour plus grated bark of *Albizia myriophylla* and says the collected Judima may be distilled into “Juhan.” This is an independent secondary documentation record that aligns botanically with SRC-335's *Albizia myriophylla* report, but its Juhan spelling and its implication that collected Judima is distilled do not resolve the SRC-304/SRC-336 feedstock disagreement. Treat it as an additional source-level account, not a botanical correction or process authority. https://www.fondazioneslowfood.com/en/ark-of-taste-slow-food/judima/ .

A Dimasa-language/cultural website, Dimasa Thairili, lists Judima, Juharo and Judi as three types of Ju but provides no distinct process for Judi. It also repeats the Acacia pennata / Thembra Humao account. This is a useful terminology lead, not enough to define Judi or establish a separate recipe. https://www.dimasathairili.com/an-overview-of-traditional-rice-wine-judima/ .

No beverage canonicalization, ingredient merge, locality edge or universal distillation method has been adopted.

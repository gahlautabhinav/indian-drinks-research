# SRC-008 full-text audit — Gogoi, Dutta & Mondal (2013)

**Audit date:** 2026-11-09  
**Source:** Barnali Gogoi, Mayurakhi Dutta & Prodyut Mondal. “Various Ethno Medicinal Plants used in the Preparation of Apong, a Traditional Beverage use by Mising Tribe of upper Assam.” *Journal of Applied Pharmaceutical Science* 3(4 Suppl 1), S85–S88 (May 2013). DOI: https://doi.org/10.7324/JAPS.2013.34.S16.  
**Publisher PDF:** https://japsonline.com/admin/php/uploads/886_pdf.pdf

## 1. Source type and scope

The article's title and abstract describe an Apong ethnobotanical review. The abstract says the authors document 39 plants and that information was collected through active field survey in three Sibsagar-district villages **and** available local literature. The article's own final citation labels it “A Review.” It should therefore be classified as a review with a field-survey component, not as a standalone 39-species primary field inventory from three villages.

The methods section states that fieldwork took place over four months, September–December 2012, in three named Mising villages: **Afola Gaon, Disang Mugh and Borhula Par**, Sibsagar district, Assam. The authors say they mainly visited Mising women, asked about starter preparation, plant parts, fermentation duration and beverage uses, and collected plant samples from nearby fields and forests with local assistance.

## 2. Locality correction

The three named villages above are the localities explicitly printed in the primary article. The previous source-register wording that placed Barmukuli village in Dhemaji was incorrect for SRC-008 and has been removed. Barmukuli/Dhemaji appears in other Apong literature and must not be imported into this source's locality field.

The article does not allocate individual plant taxa to any one of the three villages. It also does not state informant counts, household counts, village-wise sample totals or a village-by-taxon matrix. These villages are documented fieldwork sites, not proof that every village or household uses every listed plant.

## 3. Inventory count integrity

The abstract says **39 plants**. Table 1's visible serial numbers are:

- 1–14
- 16–28
- 30–39

This yields **37 displayed numbered rows**, with serial numbers 15 and 29 absent. The article does not explain the gaps. Do not silently insert two taxa, renumber the table, or report 39 independently verified table entries. Keep two distinct facts: the authors' stated total is 39; the visible table transcription contains 37 rows.

The table includes plant names, vernacular names, family labels, plant parts said to be used in Apong preparation and ethnomedicinal claims. Some entries have apparent spelling, nomenclatural or internal table-layout issues. Those should be retained as printed in a raw transcription and handled in a separate taxonomic review; they are not silently corrected here.

## 4. Herbarium and verification limits

The authors state that collected plant samples were identified with local floras, dried and deposited in the herbarium of the Department of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Dibrugarh University. However, the article gives no voucher numbers, accession identifiers, specimen list, collector numbers or public herbarium catalogue links. The deposit statement is useful but does not permit independent specimen-level verification from this paper alone.

## 5. Preparation account — evidence boundary

The paper describes collection/cleaning and drying of leaves, grinding soaked rice and plant materials separately, mixing with water, shaping oval starter cakes (approximately 6 × 3 cm), sun-drying, fumigating an earthen fermentation pot over a hearth, cooling cooked rice, adding powdered aopo pitha at a stated ratio of one cake per kilogram of rice, covering with banana leaves or *Cyclosorus exlensa* (printed spelling), fermenting for at least five days, then adding water and filtering.

Crucially, the preparation section cites Das, Deka & Miyaji (2012). Treat this as the preparation account presented by the 2013 review and likely drawn from the cited survey, not as proof that each of the three 2012 field-survey villages was directly observed following every step. It must not be used to overwrite other source-specific Apong variants or to establish a universal Mising recipe.

## 6. Ethnomedicinal claims

The article reproduces medicinal-use statements for listed plants and discusses possible pharmacological research. These are reported ethnobotanical claims and literature citations. They do not demonstrate that the finished beverage has those effects, that the plant parts are present at an effective dose after fermentation, or that the beverage is clinically safe or therapeutic.

## 7. Archive decisions

- Corrected SRC-008 locality to Afola Gaon, Disang Mugh and Borhula Par, Sibsagar district.
- Corrected source type to review with field-survey component.
- Recorded the 39-stated / 37-visible-row discrepancy.
- Recorded the missing serial numbers 15 and 29 without inventing entries.
- Recorded the herbarium-deposit claim while flagging missing voucher identifiers.
- Kept the process description separate from direct village observation because the section cites Das et al. (2012).
- No village-by-taxon records, new canonical beverage variants, or new microbial/chemical measurements created.

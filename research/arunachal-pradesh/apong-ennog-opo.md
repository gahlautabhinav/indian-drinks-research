# Arunachal Pradesh: Apong, Ennog and Nyishi Opo

## Scope and editorial status

This dossier opens the Arunachal Pradesh section of the archive. It records the broad labels **Apong** and **Ennog** described in Tiwari and Mahanta (2007), alongside the separate **Opo** entry based on a 2025 Nyishi rice-wine characterization study. These labels must not be collapsed into one canonical tradition: usage may vary by community, and the available sources do not map every product to every named village.

## Apong: source-specific process

Tiwari and Mahanta describe Apong as a white rice beer, with cooked rice mixed with a locally made starter cake called **Ipoh**, **Si-ye** or **Paa** in different tribal contexts. The mixture is placed in a covered vessel with a small amount of water and fermented; the paper reports an incubation period of **3–5 days**, after which the fermented stock is diluted with drinking water before serving.

This is a historical published process description, not a universal recipe. The authors’ broad attribution of Apong across Nyishi, Adi and Apatani communities requires more precise, community-specific confirmation. A 2025 Nyishi study uses **Opo** as the name of the beverage and its traditional starter culture. The relationship between the labels **Opo** and **Apong** should remain open pending local-language and village-level evidence.

## Ennog: black rice beer

Tiwari and Mahanta (2007) describe Ennog as a black-coloured rice beer made using cooked rice, paddy husk blackened over heat, and Ipoh starter cake. The account describes an initial stage in a conical bamboo basket called **noga-baye**, lined with leaves; the fermenting mixture is later transferred to a larger leaf-lined woven bamboo container called **Perop**. After fermentation, portions are placed in a bamboo tube called **Petok**, and hot water is poured through to collect the beverage. The paper calls the first draw **Tok-Til** and describes it as the strongest draw in that process.

The article reports roughly three days for the initial stage and about ten days overall before extraction. These durations and equipment names are specific to the published account and should not be generalised to all households or communities. The broad claim that Ennog is used by “all tribes” in the state is not accepted here as verified distribution.

## Locality evidence

Tiwari and Mahanta report ethnological fieldwork in **Tawang, West Kameng, Lower Subansiri and West Siang** districts, based on interviews with elderly villagers. Their acknowledgements name **Shyou, Hong, Ziro, Darka, Along, Poma and Itanagar**. The article does not map Apong or Ennog recipes to each individual locality; these place names are therefore recorded as a study/informant trail, not as proof of beverage production in every place.

The 2025 Opo study reports the Nyishi beverage and sample labels **Tisar, Kecha and Adak**, but the accessible repository abstract does not identify sample villages or establish whether these are stable product names, local variants or laboratory sample labels. Do not create separate canonical entries for those labels until the full text and community sources resolve their status.

## Cultural context and research safeguards

The 2007 article describes hospitality, festivals, marriages and ritual contexts in broad terms. More precise attribution by community and locality is needed before treating any practice as universal. Traditional medicinal or health-related claims in ethnological and laboratory papers are not clinical evidence of efficacy or safety.

Starter-culture knowledge is community-held. Further documentation should rely on community-authorized sources and consent-based interviews, and should not publish restricted knowledge without permission.

## Priority questions

1. What are the community-preferred names for the Nyishi beverage and starter: Opo, Apong, Ipoh, Si-ye, Paa or other local forms?
2. Are Tisar, Kecha and Adak locally recognized product variants, or labels used for samples in the 2025 study?
3. Which villages currently prepare each form, and which preparation steps differ by locality?
4. Is Ennog a distinct named beverage across several communities, and how do local practitioners describe its relationship to other black rice beverages?
5. Which cited primary sources underlie the broad distribution statements in the 2007 article?
